App blocker for students

Students are the one group this has actually been tested on, twice, at serious scale. Worth knowing what the trials found before you pick anything.

By Jacob Redshaw · 14 September 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer

An app blocker for students removes chosen apps during revision and at night. Students are the most studied group here: a 2025 randomised trial of 111 students found cutting phone use improved stress, mood and sleep, and a separate study of 45,202 students measured the same associations.

Most advice about phones and studying is guesswork. An app blocker for students is one of the few cases where there is real evidence, because students are cheap and convenient to recruit, so they are who these trials get run on.

Two studies are worth knowing about before you spend anything.

What the research on students actually found

The first is a randomised controlled trial published in BMC Medicine in 2025. It took 111 healthy students with a mean age of 22.7 and a baseline of 276 minutes of phone use a day. 58 of them were asked to hold under two hours a day for three weeks.1

Against the control group, the intervention group came out better on stress, wellbeing, depressive symptoms and sleep quality, with small to medium effect sizes.1

The second is bigger and observational. Hjetland and colleagues looked at 45,202 Norwegian students aged 18 to 28 and published in Frontiers in Psychiatry in 2025.2 Observational data cannot prove causation, so read it as a pattern across a very large group rather than proof about any individual.

Put together, the honest summary is: this has been looked at properly in your exact demographic, and using your phone less is associated with feeling better and sleeping better.

The finding that should change what you do

In the BMC Medicine trial, screen time climbed back quickly once the three weeks ended. By the six week follow up it was approaching baseline.1

This matters enormously if you are a student, because it maps exactly onto how revision goes. You go hard for the three weeks before exams, it works, and then you return to normal. Which is fine for the exam and useless for the degree.

If you want this to help across a whole term rather than a fortnight, it has to be an arrangement that survives you not caring about it.

Why phones are specifically bad for revision

Not because of willpower. Because of what revision is.

Reading something and feeling like you understood it is not learning. Retrieval is. And retrieval is effortful, uncomfortable, and offers no immediate reward, which makes it the single most interruptible activity in your day.

Anything that offers a small reward for no effort wins that competition every time. Your phone is a machine for offering exactly that.

So the phone does not just cost you the minutes you spent on it. It reliably pulls you out of the hard part and back into the easy part, which is why four hours of revision with a phone nearby can produce less than ninety minutes without one.

This is also why time spent is such a misleading measure of revision, and why so many students feel they worked hard and did badly anyway. Hours at the desk are easy to count and feel like evidence. Whether you spent those hours retrieving material or recognising it is invisible, and the phone pushes you consistently towards recognising, because recognising feels fine and retrieving feels like failing. A blocker does not make you revise well. What it does is stop the easiest available exit from being two inches from your hand, which gives the uncomfortable method a chance to actually happen.

How to set it up for a term, not a week

  1. Block the night first, not the study sessions. Counterintuitive, but sleep is doing most of the consolidation work, and a fixed bedtime block runs every night without you deciding. Blue light is a minor effect compared with simply going to bed two hours later than you meant to.
  2. Use timed sessions for revision, not all day blocks. Ninety minutes, phone apps gone, then they come back. An all day block gets switched off by Wednesday.
  3. Put your phone in another room during sessions. Free, and it does more than any app. A blocked phone in your hand is still a phone in your hand.
  4. Block the browser versions too. Blocking the app and then using the website is the most common way this quietly fails. See how to block websites on iPhone.
  5. Set it up in week one. Not the week before exams, when you have no capacity to set up systems.
Try the free version first: Apple's Screen Time Schedule costs nothing and is already on your phone. If it holds, you have solved this for free. Set it up before you pay for anything.

What an app blocker for students cannot fix

It will not make you want to revise. It removes one specific escape route, and if you are avoiding the work for reasons that have nothing to do with your phone, you will find another one. Most people do, at least at first.

It also will not help if the real problem is that you do not know how to revise. Blocking apps while you reread highlighted notes for six hours just produces a quieter version of the same ineffective method.

And if you are struggling with sleep, low mood or attention in a way that is affecting your work, that is worth taking to your university's health service rather than solving with software.

App blocker for students: FAQs

What is the best app blocker for students?

The one you will still have running in week ten. Prioritise automatic scheduling over features, because a blocker you have to start each session is the one you stop using when deadlines get heavy.

Do app blockers actually help with studying?

They remove one reliable escape route from effortful work, which helps. A 2025 randomised trial of 111 students found that cutting phone use improved stress, mood and sleep quality, though it did not measure grades.

Should I block apps at night or while revising?

Night first. Sleep does most of the consolidation work and a bedtime block runs automatically without a nightly decision. Add timed revision sessions on top of that.

Is there a free app blocker for students?

Yes. Apple's Screen Time Schedule is built into every iPhone and costs nothing. Set it up and give it two weeks before paying for anything else.

Why do I get less done with my phone nearby even when I do not use it?

Because revision is effortful and uninteresting by design, so anything offering an easy reward wins. The cost is being pulled out of the hard part, not just the minutes you spent scrolling.

Will blocking apps improve my grades?

Nobody has shown that directly. The trials measured stress, wellbeing and sleep, not results. Expect more usable study time, not a guaranteed grade, and fix your revision method as well.
