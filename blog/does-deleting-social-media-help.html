Does deleting social media help?

This has been properly tested, with payment, verification and a control group. The results are good, modest, and come with one genuine cost.

By Jacob Redshaw · 14 September 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer

Yes, modestly. Deleting social media was tested on 2,844 Facebook users, and deactivating for four weeks produced small but significant increases in wellbeing, more offline socialising and less polarisation. It also reduced factual news knowledge, and most people used Facebook much less afterwards.

Deleting social media is one of the rare bits of internet self help that has been tested with a proper randomised design rather than argued about. So you can answer the question with evidence instead of vibes.

The study worth knowing about

Allcott, Braghieri, Eichmeyer and Gentzkow published The Welfare Effects of Social Media in the American Economic Review in 2020.1

They started by measuring how much 2,844 Facebook users would need to be paid to deactivate their accounts for four weeks. A subset was then randomly assigned to actually deactivate, and the deactivation was verified rather than self reported. The window was the four weeks before the 2018 US midterm elections.

That design matters. There is a control group, the behaviour was checked, and nobody was simply asked afterwards how they felt about having used their phone less.

What got better

  • Wellbeing improved. The authors describe small but significant increases in wellbeing.1 Small is the operative word. This was not transformational.
  • Offline life increased. People spent more time on offline activities, including socialising with family and friends, and also watching television alone.1 Worth noting that the replacement activity was not uniformly noble.
  • Political polarisation fell. A measured reduction.1
  • The effect outlasted the study. There was a large and persistent drop in Facebook use after the experiment ended, and people valued Facebook less afterwards than before.1

That last point is the most interesting thing in the paper. Four weeks away permanently changed how much people wanted the thing. Most interventions do not survive their own ending.

It also quietly undermines the usual defence of these products. If people value something less after being made to live without it for a month, then their enthusiasm beforehand was partly a product of the habit rather than a judgement about the thing itself. The authors make this point in economic terms, noting that standard measures may overstate how much benefit users actually get. In plainer language: you probably like it less than you think you do, and the only way to find out is to stop for long enough to see.

What got worse

One finding, and it is real. Deactivation reduced factual news knowledge.1

Does deleting social media help more than cutting back?

Different question, and the comparison is imperfect because the studies are not the same.

A 2025 randomised controlled trial in BMC Medicine took a different approach: 111 students averaging 276 minutes of phone use a day, with 58 asked to hold under two hours a day for three weeks. Against the control group they improved on stress, wellbeing, depressive symptoms and sleep quality, with small to medium effect sizes.2

So reducing works too. But here is the contrast that should decide what you do.

In the reduction trial, screen time climbed back quickly once the limit ended and was approaching baseline by the six week follow up.2 In the deactivation study, the drop in use persisted after the experiment finished.1

Deleting appears to change your relationship with the thing. Limiting appears to pause it. That is a meaningful difference, and it argues for deletion being the stronger move if you can face it.

A sensible way to test it on yourself

  1. Pick one app, not all of them. The worst one. Deleting everything at once is the version you abandon.
  2. Two weeks, not forever. Forever is too big to agree to, which is why people never start. Two weeks is long enough to break the reflex.
  3. Delete the app, do not deactivate the account. You keep your messages and your photos. The reflex is tied to the icon, not the account.
  4. Decide where your news comes from first. Pick a newspaper, a newsletter, a radio bulletin. Do this before you delete, because of the knowledge finding above.
  5. Plan the gaps. You reach for it in queues, adverts and the ten minutes before sleep. Have something else within reach or you will reinstall by day four.
  6. Keep the browser out too. Using the website instead of the app is the most common way this fails.
The honest expectation: small but real improvements in how you feel, more time with people, slightly worse awareness of the news, and a decent chance you use it much less even after you reinstall.

If you cannot delete it

Plenty of people genuinely cannot. Work accounts, a business that runs on Instagram, a family group that lives on one app. Deletion is not available to you and that is a real constraint rather than an excuse.

In that case the useful move is removing it at specific times rather than permanently. The hours before bed are the cheapest to give up and the ones with the least to defend them. That is a timing problem, and it is what a screen time blocker is for.

Deleting social media: FAQs

Does deleting social media improve mental health?

Modestly. A randomised study of 2,844 Facebook users found small but significant increases in wellbeing after four weeks of verified deactivation, along with more offline socialising and less political polarisation.

Is there a downside to quitting social media?

Yes. The same study found deactivation reduced factual news knowledge. If a feed is your main news source, choose a deliberate replacement before you delete it.

How long should I delete social media for?

Two weeks is a good test. It is long enough to break the reflex and short enough that you will actually start, unlike a permanent commitment you keep postponing.

Is deleting better than just limiting my use?

The evidence leans that way. Deactivation produced a persistent drop in use after the study ended, while a separate reduction trial saw usage return towards baseline once the limit stopped.

Should I delete the app or deactivate the account?

Delete the app first. You keep your messages and photos, and the habit is attached to the icon on your home screen rather than to the account itself.

What if I cannot delete it for work?

Then make it unavailable at specific times instead of permanently. The hours before bed are the easiest to give up and the ones that cost you the most sleep.
