What is nomophobia?

A word with a dubious origin describing something genuinely real. Both halves of that are worth separating out.

By Jacob Redshaw · 14 September 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer

Nomophobia is the anxiety or discomfort of being without your phone or unable to use it. The word is a contraction of no mobile phone phobia. It is not a recognised diagnosis in ICD-11 or the DSM-5, and the discomfort it describes is nonetheless common and measurable.

Nomophobia means the anxiety of being without your phone, or of being unable to use it. The word is a contraction of no mobile phone phobia, and it came from a commissioned survey rather than from clinical research, which is worth knowing before anyone quotes a percentage at you.

But the thing it describes is real, and most people reading this have felt it.

Is nomophobia a real diagnosis?

No. It does not appear as a disorder in the World Health Organization's ICD-11, and it is not in the DSM-5 either. No doctor can diagnose you with it, because there is nothing to diagnose you with.

Worth noting what the WHO does recognise in this territory, because it shows where the bar actually sits. ICD-11 includes gaming disorder, defined as a pattern of gaming behaviour characterised by impaired control over gaming, increasing priority given to gaming over other activities to the extent that gaming takes precedence over other interests and daily activities, and continuation or escalation of gaming despite the occurrence of negative consequences.1

The WHO also requires that the pattern significantly impairs personal, family, social, educational or occupational functioning, and that it would normally have been evident for at least 12 months.1

That is the threshold for the closest recognised condition. Feeling uneasy when you leave your phone at home does not come near it, and that is reassuring rather than dismissive.

Also, it is probably not a phobia

Clinical phobias involve disproportionate fear of something that poses little actual threat, usually with avoidance behaviour and a strong physical response.

Being without your phone is not like that. The discomfort is mostly proportionate, because the phone really does hold your means of contacting people, navigating, paying for things and proving who you are. Feeling exposed without it is a sensible reaction to carrying one object that does all of that.

So the name does the subject a disservice. It frames an ordinary dependency as an irrational fear, which makes people feel defective about something that is largely a design consequence.

What the discomfort is usually about

Three separate things get bundled under one word, and they call for different responses.

Practical dependency. Your tickets, your banking authentication, your maps, your two factor codes. This is not anxiety, it is accurate risk assessment. Carrying a card and writing down an address genuinely reduces it.

Social availability. The worry that someone needs you and cannot reach you. Often reasonable, occasionally not. Worth asking how often anyone has actually needed you urgently in the last year.

The absence of distraction. This is the one that matters. Without a phone you are left with your own thoughts in queues, on walks and before sleep, and if those thoughts are unwelcome then the phone has been doing a job you had not named. Removing it feels worse than it should, because you are not losing entertainment, you are losing avoidance.

If the third one is your version, no blocker fixes it, and treating it as a discipline problem will not work either.

What the evidence supports

Reducing phone use helps, modestly. A 2025 randomised controlled trial in BMC Medicine took 111 students averaging 276 minutes a day and had 58 hold under two hours a day for three weeks. Against the control group they improved on stress, wellbeing, depressive symptoms and sleep quality, with small to medium effect sizes.2

And there is a relevant finding about leaving a service entirely. A randomised study of 2,844 Facebook users found four weeks of verified deactivation produced small wellbeing gains and a large persistent drop in use afterwards, with people valuing the service less than they had before.3

That last detail speaks directly to nomophobia. The anticipated discomfort of being without something is reliably worse than the actual experience of it, and the only way to find that out is to try.

Where the figures you have seen come from

You will find claims that some large percentage of people suffer from nomophobia. Treat all of them carefully.

Most trace back to commissioned surveys rather than to clinical assessment, and they measure agreement with statements like feeling uncomfortable without your phone. That is a reasonable thing to measure and it is not the same as a condition. Asking people whether they would feel uneasy without an object holding their money, keys to their accounts and route home will produce a high number regardless of anybody's mental health.

There are also questionnaires in the academic literature attempting to measure this more carefully, and they are genuinely useful for research. What none of them do is establish a threshold above which you have something. There is no agreed cut off, because there is no agreed condition.

A test that costs nothing

Leave the phone at home for one short, low stakes trip. A walk, the shop, an hour out.

Pay attention to the first ten minutes, which is where the discomfort lives, and then to the following fifty, which is usually fine. If the whole hour is genuinely distressing rather than mildly odd, that is worth mentioning to a GP, because generalised anxiety presents exactly this way and is treatable.

The short version: not a diagnosis, not really a phobia, and the uneasy feeling is usually either practical dependency or the loss of a distraction you had not noticed you were using.

Nomophobia: FAQs

What does nomophobia mean?

The anxiety or discomfort of being without your phone or unable to use it. The word is a contraction of no mobile phone phobia and originated in a commissioned survey rather than clinical research.

Is nomophobia a recognised condition?

No. It is not in ICD-11 or the DSM-5, so it cannot be formally diagnosed. The discomfort it describes is still common and worth taking seriously on its own terms.

Is nomophobia actually a phobia?

Probably not. Clinical phobias involve disproportionate fear of something largely harmless. Feeling exposed without a device holding your money, maps and identity is a fairly proportionate reaction.

Why does being without my phone feel so bad?

Usually one of three things: practical dependency on what it holds, worry that someone cannot reach you, or the loss of a distraction from your own thoughts.

How do I test whether I have a problem?

Leave it at home for one short low stakes trip. The discomfort usually lives in the first ten minutes. If a whole hour is genuinely distressing, mention it to a GP.

Does using my phone less help?

Modestly, yes. A 2025 randomised trial found improvements in stress, mood and sleep when students cut to under two hours a day, with effects described as small to medium.
