Why apps are addictive: the design tricks that keep you hooked

You open an app for one thing and surface twenty minutes later. That is not an accident. Here is how the design works, and how to push back.

By Jacob Redshaw · 14 September 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer

Why apps are addictive comes down to business and design. Many free apps earn money from your attention and data, so they are built to keep you using them. Features like infinite scroll, unpredictable rewards, notifications, autoplay, streaks and likes remove natural stopping points and give you reasons to come back.

Why apps are addictive: the short version

To understand why apps are addictive, start with how they make money. Montag and colleagues explain that the classic Silicon Valley app model asks you to pay with your personal data rather than cash. So developers have a strong interest in keeping you in the app for as long as possible, because more time means more data and better targeted ads.1

Aza Raskin, who designed infinite scroll in 2006, made the same point to the BBC in 2018. He said that to get the next round of funding or push a stock price up, "the amount of time that people spend on your app has to go up."2

This is often called the attention economy. A 2021 study of YouTube describes video apps in the attention economy using mechanisms like autoplay to maximise watch time.3

What is persuasive design?

Persuasive design is the use of psychology in a product's design to steer what you do, such as how often you open an app and how long you stay. It is a field of study in computer science, and Montag's team link many app features to classic psychological effects.1

None of these features is sinister on its own. Together, they make stopping harder than starting.

6 addictive app design techniques

1. Infinite scroll

Infinite scroll lets you keep swiping through content without ever clicking to a next page. Montag and colleagues describe how you become more and more absorbed without reaching a natural stop where you might reconsider.1

Raskin told the BBC the feature kept people looking at their phones far longer than necessary. He said he had not set out to addict people and felt guilty about it.2

2. Variable rewards

Most of what you scroll past is forgettable. Now and then you hit something funny or interesting. Montag's team say this follows intermittent conditioning, the same principle researchers study in slot machines.1 Sandy Parakilas, a former Facebook employee, also compared social media to a slot machine in the BBC report.2

3. Notifications

Notifications pull you back in when you were doing something else. In a small study, 30 volunteers turned off notification alerts on all their devices for 24 hours. They felt less distracted and more productive, but some felt anxious about not replying as quickly as expected, and less connected to their social group.4

Social pressure adds to it. Montag's team point to read receipts on WhatsApp: once both people know a message has been read, both expect a fast reply.1

4. Autoplay

When one video ends, the next begins. Montag's team note this happens on YouTube, Netflix and Amazon Prime, and that it makes it hard to stop watching.1 In the YouTube study, a survey of 120 US users found autoplay and recommendations mainly undermined their sense of agency, while search and playlists supported it.3

5. Streaks

On Snapchat, a Streak counts how many days in a row you and a friend have sent each other Snaps. An hourglass emoji warns you when a Streak is about to expire, and if more than a day passes without a Snap from everyone, it ends.5

A long Streak can start to feel like something you own. Montag's team describe a related effect in games: the more time you invest in building something, the harder it is to walk away.1

6. Likes and social validation

Likes are feedback you can count. In a brain imaging study of 34 adolescents aged 13 to 18, viewing photos with many likes, compared with few, was linked to greater activity in brain regions involved in reward processing and attention. The teenagers were also more likely to like photos that already had many likes.6

Leah Pearlman, co inventor of Facebook's Like button, told the BBC she had come to base her sense of self worth on the number of likes she got.2

Are apps really addictive, or is that hype?

It is worth being fair. Montag's team call their paper theoretical and say it is speculative to assume every feature was designed purely to extend use.1 In the BBC report, Facebook said wanting something to be addictive plays no part in how it designs products, and Snap denied using visual tricks.2

"Smartphone addiction" is also not a recognised diagnosis.1 Our guide to phone addiction signs explains why many researchers say problematic smartphone use instead. Either way, knowing how these features work makes them easier to spot.

How to push back against addictive app design

You cannot redesign the apps, but you can change how they reach you.

  • Turn off non essential notifications, and move social media apps into a folder off your home screen. Both were strategies in a 2022 trial that reduced problematic smartphone use and screen time.7
  • Switch off autoplay in the apps that let you.
  • Search instead of browsing the feed, which supported people's sense of control in the YouTube study.3
  • Put the stopping point back yourself with scheduled blocks. For doomscrolling at night, see how to stop doomscrolling.

Why apps are addictive: FAQs

Why are apps so addictive?

Many free apps earn money from attention and data, so they use design features like infinite scroll, unpredictable rewards, notifications, autoplay, streaks and likes that make it easy to keep going and hard to stop.

What is infinite scroll and why is it addictive?

Infinite scroll loads new content as you swipe, so there is no end of the page. Without a natural stopping point, you are less likely to pause and decide to leave.

What are variable rewards in apps?

Variable rewards are payoffs that arrive unpredictably, like a great post buried among ordinary ones. Researchers compare this intermittent reinforcement to the way slot machines keep people playing.

What is the attention economy?

The attention economy describes businesses competing for your time and attention, often because attention and data can be sold to advertisers. Apps in it are designed to maximise how long you use them.

Who invented infinite scroll?

The BBC reported that Aza Raskin designed infinite scroll in 2006 while working at the consultancy Humanized. He later said he felt guilty about how it kept people on their phones.

How can I make apps less addictive?

Turn off non essential notifications, switch off autoplay, move social apps off your home screen and use search rather than the feed. Scheduled app blocks add back the stopping point the design removes.
