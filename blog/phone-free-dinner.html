The phone free dinner

The rule is easy to announce and hard to keep, because it turns one person into the police. Here is the version that does not.

By Jacob Redshaw · 14 September 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer

A phone free dinner works best when the phones are physically elsewhere rather than governed by a rule at the table. Put them in another room before you sit down, and block your own apps automatically for that window so nobody has to police anybody.

Almost everybody agrees a phone free dinner is a good idea, and almost nobody sustains one. The reason is structural rather than moral.

Why the rule version fails

A rule at the table needs enforcing, and enforcing it makes one person the police.

That person now has a choice every time somebody reaches for their phone: say something and be the nag, or say nothing and watch the rule dissolve. Both options cost them, and after a fortnight they stop paying.

There is a second problem. The rule arrives at the moment of temptation, which is the worst possible time for any rule. Somebody has already picked the phone up, and asking them to put it down now is a confrontation rather than an arrangement.

So the useful move is the same as everywhere else in this subject: decide in advance, and make the decision physical rather than verbal.

The phone free dinner version that actually works

  1. A bowl or a shelf by the kitchen door. Phones go there on the way in, not at the table. One place, always the same place.
  2. Everyone does it, including you, especially you. If the person who proposed the rule keeps their phone for a legitimate reason, the arrangement is over on the first evening.
  3. Block your own apps for the window automatically. Not because the phone is in your hand, but because it removes the temptation to go and get it between courses. A scheduled block from 18:30 to 19:30 costs you nothing and nobody has to notice it.
  4. Decide the genuine exception in advance. Somebody on call, an expected call from a hospital, a child out somewhere. Name it, allow it, and then everybody knows that a phone at the table means something real rather than an argument.
  5. Do not announce it as a policy. Just start putting yours in the bowl. Rules imposed on a household get resisted. A habit the household drifts into does not.

Why dinner specifically is worth protecting

It is the one reliably shared hour most households have, and it is short. You are not asking anyone to give up something they value for a long stretch, which makes it the cheapest possible win.

It is also the hour where the cost of a phone is most visible to other people. An hour lost alone in the evening costs you. An hour of half attention at a table costs everybody there, and they notice, even when nobody says anything.

There is a practical reason to start here too. Because dinner has a natural beginning and end, it is one of the few parts of the day where a block has an obvious shape. You do not have to decide how long, or negotiate with yourself about whether tonight counts. The meal starts, the meal finishes, and the arrangement is over. Compare that with trying to use your phone less in general, which has no edges and therefore no way of being either kept or broken.

Worth being clear that I cannot point you to a trial showing phone free meals improve relationships. Nobody has run it properly, as far as I can find, and anyone quoting a figure for it is inventing one.

What has been measured is adjacent and mildly supportive. A randomised study of 2,844 Facebook users found that four weeks of verified deactivation increased offline activity, including socialising with family and friends.1 So time taken off a phone does partly go to people, at least some of it.

Children, honestly

If this is about your kids rather than about you, two things are worth saying.

The first is that a rule you do not follow yourself does not work, and that is not a moral point. Children calibrate against behaviour rather than instruction, so the arrangement either applies to the adults or it does not exist.

The second is that Apple's family controls are a genuinely different tool from a self restriction and are the right one for a child's device. Those are set in Settings, then Family, then your child's name, and approvals are held by you rather than by them.2 That is enforceable in a way nothing you install on your own phone can be.

If you live alone

The same logic applies and the stakes are different. Eating while scrolling is the most reliable way to finish a meal without having tasted it, and it tends to extend the session well past the plate.

Phone in another room, meal at a table rather than at a screen. It costs twenty minutes and it is one of the few changes in this subject that is purely a gain.

If eating alone in silence is genuinely unpleasant, which for a lot of people it is, pick a replacement rather than pretending you will enjoy the quiet. Radio, music, a book propped against something. The aim is not austerity. It is that the thing accompanying your meal should be one that ends, instead of one engineered to carry you from dinner into the rest of the evening. For the general version of that argument see why you cannot stop scrolling.

Start tonight: put a bowl by the kitchen door, put yours in it first, and say nothing about it for a week.

Phone free dinner: FAQs

How do I make a phone free dinner work?

Put the phones somewhere physical before you sit down rather than making a rule at the table. A bowl by the kitchen door works, because then nobody has to enforce anything on anybody.

Why do no phones at the table rules fail?

Because they need enforcing, which makes one person the police. That person either becomes the nag or lets it slide, and after a couple of weeks they stop paying the cost.

Should there be exceptions?

Yes, named in advance. Somebody on call or waiting for news from a hospital. Agreeing the exception beforehand means a phone at the table signals something real instead of starting an argument.

Does a phone free dinner improve relationships?

No trial has shown it, and anyone quoting a figure is inventing one. A randomised deactivation study did find that time off social media partly went into socialising with family and friends.

How do I handle this with children?

The rule has to apply to the adults or it does not exist, because children calibrate against behaviour rather than instruction. For a child's device, Apple's family controls are the right tool.

Does it matter if I live alone?

The same logic applies. Eating while scrolling is the most reliable way to finish a meal without tasting it, and it usually extends the session long past the plate.
