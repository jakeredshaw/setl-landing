Is phone addiction real?

Two things are true. Phone addiction is not a clinical diagnosis anywhere. And the thing people mean by it is measurable and common.

By Jacob Redshaw · 14 September 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer

Phone addiction is not a recognised diagnosis in either ICD-11 or the DSM-5. The World Health Organization does recognise gaming disorder, with specific criteria. So phone addiction is real as a described problem and not real as a medical label, and both parts matter.

Ask whether phone addiction is real and you get two confident answers, both wrong, depending on who you ask. One says it is a modern epidemic rewiring your brain. The other says it is moral panic and people said the same about novels.

The accurate answer is less satisfying and more useful.

What is actually recognised

Phone addiction is not a diagnosis. It does not appear as a disorder in the World Health Organization's ICD-11, and it is not in the DSM-5 either. No doctor can diagnose you with it, because there is nothing to diagnose you with.

What the WHO does recognise is gaming disorder. ICD-11 defines it as a pattern of gaming behaviour characterised by impaired control over gaming, increasing priority given to gaming over other activities to the extent that gaming takes precedence over other interests and daily activities, and continuation or escalation of gaming despite the occurrence of negative consequences.1

The WHO adds two qualifiers that get dropped whenever this is quoted. The pattern must be severe enough to significantly impair personal, family, social, educational or occupational functioning, and it would normally have been evident for at least 12 months.1

The DSM-5 went further and declined to recognise it at all for now, listing Internet Gaming Disorder only as a condition for further study.

So even for gaming, which is the closest thing to a recognised digital addiction, the two main manuals disagree. For phones specifically, there is no formal category in either.

It is worth understanding why the bar is set there rather than assuming the manuals are simply behind. A diagnosis has consequences. It shapes who gets treatment, what insurers pay for, and what gets researched, so adding one too early does real damage if the category turns out to be wrong. Gaming disorder itself drew strong objections, including from researchers who thought the evidence was not yet good enough to justify it. That caution is a feature, not negligence, and it is the reason absence from a manual tells you very little about whether your own phone use is costing you something.

Why that does not settle it

Absence from a manual is not evidence that nothing is happening. Diagnostic categories are conservative by design, they move slowly, and they exist mainly so clinicians can agree on treatment. Plenty of real difficulties have no code.

There is also a reasonable argument against making this a diagnosis, which is that it would medicalise something close to universal. If most adults in a developed country show the behaviour, a diagnosis stops distinguishing anything useful.

What you can do is look at measured associations in large samples rather than at labels. Hjetland and colleagues studied 45,202 Norwegian students aged 18 to 28 and published the results in Frontiers in Psychiatry in 2025.2 That is a serious sample size, and it tells you more about the real world than any argument about terminology.

A self test that is actually grounded in something

Since there is no phone addiction checklist, borrow the WHO's gaming criteria, which were written by people who thought carefully about where the line sits. Swap gaming for your phone and ask three questions honestly.

  1. Impaired control. Do you repeatedly use it for much longer than you intended, and stop later than you meant to, most days?
  2. Priority over other things. Has it started displacing things you say matter more, such as sleep, exercise, work or the people in the room?
  3. Continuation despite harm. Has it already cost you something real, and did you carry on anyway?

Then apply the WHO's two qualifiers, because they are what separate a bad habit from a disorder. Is it significantly impairing your functioning, and has it been going on for about a year?1

Most people answer yes to the three questions and no to the qualifiers. That combination is worth naming clearly: a genuine problem, worth fixing, that is not a clinical condition. You do not need a diagnosis to justify doing something about it.

Why the phone addiction label matters less than you think

Here is the practical point. Whether or not it is an addiction, the thing that helps is the same.

A 2025 randomised controlled trial in BMC Medicine took 111 students averaging 276 minutes of phone use a day, and had 58 of them hold under two hours a day for three weeks. Against the control group they improved on stress, wellbeing, depressive symptoms and sleep quality.3

None of those students were diagnosed with anything. The intervention was not treatment. It was just using the phone less, and it worked anyway.

And the detail that should shape what you do next: usage returned towards baseline once the limit stopped.3 Whatever you call the problem, the improvement lasts as long as the arrangement does.

When to see a doctor: if phone use is genuinely impairing your work, sleep or relationships, or you are using it to manage low mood or anxiety, that is worth raising with a GP regardless of what it is called. Underlying depression, anxiety and ADHD are all treatable and all commonly sit underneath this.

Phone addiction: FAQs

Is phone addiction a real medical condition?

No. It is not a diagnosis in ICD-11 or the DSM-5, so no doctor can formally diagnose it. That does not make the underlying problem imaginary, and you do not need a diagnosis to decide to change it.

Does the WHO recognise phone addiction?

The WHO recognises gaming disorder in ICD-11, not phone or social media addiction. Its criteria are impaired control, gaming taking priority over other activities, and continuing despite negative consequences.

How long does a problem have to last to be a disorder?

For gaming disorder, the WHO says the pattern would normally have been evident for at least 12 months, and must significantly impair personal, family, social, educational or occupational functioning.

Why is phone addiction not in the DSM-5?

The DSM-5 took a cautious position on digital behaviours generally, listing Internet Gaming Disorder only as a condition needing further study. Phone use specifically has no category in it.

If it is not an addiction, why can I not stop?

Because feeds are built without stopping points, so continuing is the default and you have to interrupt yourself repeatedly. That is a design problem rather than evidence of a disorder.

Should I see a doctor about my phone use?

Worth it if it is genuinely affecting your work, sleep or relationships, or if you use it to manage low mood or anxiety. Depression, anxiety and ADHD often sit underneath and are all treatable.
