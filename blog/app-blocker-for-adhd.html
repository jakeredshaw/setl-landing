App blocker for ADHD: does blocking apps actually help?

Phones and ADHD can be a hard mix. Here is what the evidence says, what blocking apps can and cannot do, and how to set it up well.

By Jacob Redshaw · 14 September 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer

An app blocker for ADHD does not treat ADHD, but it can help you manage distraction. It removes tempting apps at times you choose in advance, so you rely less on willpower in the moment. Research links ADHD symptoms with problematic social media use, and a large US study found firm screen time limits reduced app use.

Can an app blocker for ADHD help you focus?

An app blocker for ADHD will not treat ADHD. What it can do is change your surroundings, so the apps that pull you off track are simply not available for a while.

An app blocker is a tool that stops chosen apps from opening during times you set in advance. For a lot of people, that small change works better than another promise to just be more disciplined.

This guide covers what research says about ADHD, phones and sleep, why removing the option tends to beat willpower, and how to set up a blocker so it sticks.

ADHD and phone use: what does the research say?

The NHS lists being easily distracted or forgetful, and finding it hard to organise your time, among the signs of ADHD in adults.1 A phone full of feeds and notifications offers an endless supply of new things to switch to.

Researchers have studied the link between ADHD and problematic phone and social media use. A 2025 meta analysis pooled 15 studies with 35,223 participants and found a moderate positive correlation between ADHD symptoms and problematic social media use.2

A 2022 meta analysis looked at 14 studies covering 2,488 children and adolescents aged 6 to 18. Those diagnosed with ADHD showed more severe problematic internet use than peers without a clinical diagnosis.3

Links, not causes: These studies show associations. They do not show that phones cause ADHD, or that ADHD always leads to heavy phone use. They do suggest the pull you feel is something researchers take seriously.

Is ADHD phone addiction a real thing?

The studies above use the term problematic use rather than addiction. It focuses on whether your use is causing problems in your life. If you want to check your own habits, our guide to signs of phone addiction walks through them.

ADHD and sleep: why nights are often the hardest part

Sleep and ADHD are closely tied. A 2019 review reported that delayed sleep phase disorder, where your body clock runs late, is found in 73 to 78% of children and adults with ADHD.4

The same review is careful about direction. ADHD may cause sleep problems, sleep problems may add to ADHD symptoms, or both may share a common background.4

NHS advice for adults with ADHD is to have a regular bedtime and a quiet, dark bedroom, and to try to avoid screens, caffeine, sugar and alcohol close to bedtime.1

That is where the phone gets tricky. If your body clock already runs late, an open feed at 11pm makes it even easier to keep going. We look at this pattern in our guide to bedtime procrastination.

Why removing the option beats willpower

Most advice about phone distraction boils down to trying harder. ADHD organisations and self control research both point somewhere else.

CHADD, the US organisation Children and Adults with ADHD, notes that many adults with ADHD point to their smartphones as a major cause of procrastination. Its suggestions include turning your phone off, using do not disturb, and using built in app timers or app blocking settings.5

Psychologists Angela Duckworth, Tamar Szabó Gendler and James Gross argue that situational strategies, which change your surroundings before a temptation grows, may be especially effective. One of their examples is a student who goes to the library without a phone.6

They also make a useful point. Because these strategies stop the struggle before it starts, people may underrate how well they work.6

A large experiment tested this with real phones. Economists recruited about 2,000 US adults with Android phones and offered some of them screen time limits that in most cases could not be immediately overridden. Those limits cut use of six tempting apps, including Facebook, Instagram and YouTube, by 22 minutes a day, or 16%, over 12 weeks.7

That study was not about ADHD. But the principle carries over: a decision made in advance is easier to keep than one made with the feed already open.

Why this may matter more with ADHD

Willpower asks you to make the right call in the moment, again and again. If distraction and time management are already hard for you, that moment is the weakest point. A blocker moves the decision earlier, to when you are calm and planning.

How to set up an ADHD focus app that sticks

  1. Start with one problem time. Pick the moment that costs you most, like late nights or the first hour of work. Do not try to fix your whole day at once.
  2. Block apps, not the phone. Keep calls, maps, music and the messages you truly need. Block the feeds and games that swallow time.
  3. Decide in advance. Set the schedule when you are calm, so nothing depends on a choice at 11pm.
  4. Make quitting slower than one tap. Limits you can dismiss instantly are easy to ignore. Add friction so breaking the plan takes a deliberate step.
  5. Keep focus blocks short at first. A 25 minute block you finish beats a four hour block you abandon.
  6. Add a physical change. Charge your phone away from your bed, so it is not the last thing you reach for at night.
  7. Plan the exceptions. Night shifts, parties and deadlines happen. Planning them ahead stops one late night from undoing the habit.

ADHD screen time: use the data you already have

Your iPhone already tracks screen time. On iOS 27, open Settings, tap Screen Time, then tap Daily Average to see daily or weekly use, plus how often you pick up your phone.8

Look for patterns rather than totals. Which apps, and which hours, cause the most trouble? If you have tried built in limits before and they did not hold, our piece on why screen time limits stop working explains why.

What an app blocker for ADHD cannot do

  • It does not treat ADHD or replace assessment, medication, therapy or coaching.
  • It will not fix sleep problems that have a medical cause.
  • It cannot make a boring task interesting. It only removes one easy escape.

If attention, sleep or mood are affecting your life, talk to your GP. The NHS notes that people with ADHD often have other conditions too, such as depression, anxiety or addictions.1

App blocker for ADHD: FAQs

Do app blockers help with ADHD?

They can help some people manage distraction by removing tempting apps at set times. They do not treat ADHD. A large US study found firm screen time limits reduced use of tempting apps, but it was not a study of people with ADHD.

What is the best app blocker for ADHD?

The best one is the one you keep using. Look for scheduled blocks, limits that take a deliberate step to break, and a way to plan exceptions. Start with one problem time, such as bedtime.

Is phone addiction more common in people with ADHD?

Studies link ADHD symptoms with problematic social media and internet use, including a 2025 meta analysis of 15 studies. These are associations, so they do not show that one causes the other.

Why do people with ADHD struggle with sleep?

Reasons vary. A 2019 review reported delayed sleep phase disorder in 73 to 78% of children and adults with ADHD, and said the link may run both ways. Speak to your GP about ongoing sleep problems.

Should I use Screen Time or an app blocker for ADHD?

Try what you already have first. Apple's Screen Time shows your daily use and pickups. If limits are too easy to dismiss in the moment, a blocker that adds friction may suit you better.
