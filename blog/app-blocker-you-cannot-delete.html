Is there an app blocker you cannot delete?

The honest answer is no, and anyone promising otherwise is lying to you. What matters is how slow the undoing is.

By Jacob Redshaw · 14 September 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer

No. There is no app blocker you cannot delete on iPhone, because Apple requires every app to be removable and gives third party apps no power to prevent their own deletion. The best any blocker can do is make undoing slow and deliberate rather than instant.

People search for an app blocker you cannot delete for an entirely sensible reason. They have tried blockers, they have deleted them at 1am, and they want something that takes the choice away properly.

The honest answer is that it does not exist on iPhone, and it cannot. Here is why, and what to look for instead.

Why an app blocker you cannot delete is impossible on iPhone

Three structural reasons, none of which any developer can engineer around.

Apple requires apps to be removable. You can delete any third party app from an iPhone. A developer cannot opt out of that, and an app that tried would not pass review.

Blockers run on permissions you grant. Third party blockers are built on Apple's Screen Time framework, and that permission is yours. You granted it, so you can revoke it, from Settings, without involving the app at all.

You can reset the phone. At the absolute limit, erase and restore. Drastic, and available.

So any product claiming it cannot be removed is either describing a parental controls setup on someone else's device, or it is not telling you the truth. Treat that claim as a reason for suspicion rather than a selling point.

Why Apple built it that way

It is worth understanding rather than resenting, because it explains the whole category.

An app that could genuinely lock you out of your own phone, permanently, with no escape, would be a serious liability. It would be used for coercive control. It would trap people whose circumstances change. It would create a device somebody else can hold hostage.

So the escape hatch is deliberate. It is not an oversight that developers are working on. It is the design, and it is the correct design, even though it is inconvenient for the specific person who genuinely wants to be stopped.

What to look for instead of undeletable

The useful question is not can I remove it. It is how long does removing it take, and will the urge survive that long.

Most late night relapses last a couple of minutes. A blocker that costs thirty seconds of deliberate effort to escape defeats most of them. One that costs a single tap defeats none.

So judge blockers on these, in order.

  1. How many seconds to undo. A press and hold of twenty or thirty seconds is a genuinely different thing from a tap. You have to stand there, deliberately, deciding, which is exactly the state the urge cannot survive.
  2. Does the undo require attention. Something you can do absent mindedly is not friction. Something that requires you to keep your finger in place and watch a timer is.
  3. Does it run without you. A block you have to start is one you forget on the night it matters. Automatic schedules beat manual sessions for exactly this reason.
  4. Is there a planned way out. This one is counterintuitive and it is the most important. If the only way to handle a wedding is to disable the whole system, you will disable it, and then you will not switch it back on. A blocker with a built in way to book a night off is harder to abandon than a stricter one without.

The free thing that beats all of it

Physical distance. Charge the phone in another room and buy a ten pound alarm clock.

This is the only genuinely hard block available to you, because it does not run on permissions you can revoke. It runs on the fact that the phone is downstairs. There is no settings menu for that.

Anyone selling you software should tell you this first, and most do not.

A second option in the same spirit is handing the Screen Time passcode to somebody else. Not as a punishment arrangement, just as a practical one: a partner or a friend sets it, does not tell you, and you ask them if you genuinely need it changed. That converts a four second override into a conversation, and most urges do not survive having to involve another person. It works for the same reason the other room works, which is that it moves the barrier outside the device.

What the evidence says about strictness

There is a reason not to chase maximum strictness. A 2025 randomised controlled trial in BMC Medicine had 58 students hold under two hours of phone a day for three weeks, with measured improvements in stress, wellbeing, depressive symptoms and sleep quality against a control group. Then screen time climbed back towards baseline once the three weeks ended.1

The benefit lasted as long as the arrangement did. Which means the thing you actually need is not the strictest possible block. It is the strictest block you will still be using in March.

Those are different, and people consistently buy the first and need the second. A harsh setup you abandon in three weeks loses to a moderate one that runs for a year.

The honest summary: nothing on iOS is undeletable. Aim for slow to undo, automatic to start, and survivable when life gets in the way.

App blocker you cannot delete: FAQs

Is there an app blocker that cannot be deleted on iPhone?

No. Apple requires every third party app to be removable, and blockers run on a Screen Time permission you granted and can revoke. Any product claiming otherwise is not being straight with you.

Why will Apple not allow an undeletable blocker?

Because an app that could permanently lock someone out of their own phone would be used for coercive control and would trap people whose circumstances change. The escape hatch is deliberate.

What is the strictest app blocker for iPhone?

Judge by how long undoing takes rather than by claims of strictness. A twenty or thirty second deliberate hold defeats most late night urges. A single tap defeats none.

Can I stop myself turning off Screen Time?

Only partly. Use a random passcode you have not memorised, written down and stored somewhere inconvenient. Anyone who knows the passcode can disable everything in seconds.

What actually works better than software?

Charging the phone in another room with a cheap alarm clock. It is the only genuinely hard block available, because it does not depend on a permission you can revoke.

Should I get the strictest blocker I can find?

Probably not. In the one trial that followed up, benefits faded as soon as the arrangement stopped. A moderate setup you keep for a year beats a harsh one you abandon in three weeks.
