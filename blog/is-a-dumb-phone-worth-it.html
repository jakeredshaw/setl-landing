Is a dumb phone worth it?

It genuinely works, it costs more than money, and there is a cheaper experiment that tells you most of what you need to know first.

By Jacob Redshaw · 14 September 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer

A dumb phone works because it removes the capability rather than asking you to resist it, which is why nothing can be undone at midnight. The cost is navigation, banking authentication, app only tickets, photos and group messaging. Run a two week stripped down test first.

The case for a dumb phone is simple and strong: you cannot scroll on a device that cannot scroll. No settings to adjust, no app to delete, no passcode you know by heart.

That is a real advantage and it is the only intervention in this whole subject that cannot be undone in the moment. Worth taking seriously.

But the trade is bigger than people expect, and there is a cheaper test to run first.

What you actually give up

Not social media. You wanted to give that up. The problem is everything else that quietly moved onto the phone over fifteen years.

  • Navigation. You will need to plan journeys in advance, or buy a satnav, or get lost occasionally. This is the one people underestimate most.
  • Banking and payments. Many banks now require an app for authentication, so this can affect your ability to log in anywhere, not just to pay for things.
  • Tickets and boarding passes. Increasingly app only. Printing is sometimes possible and sometimes not.
  • Group messaging. If your friends and family organise on a messaging app, you become the person who has to be told separately. This has a social cost that falls on other people, which is worth being honest about.
  • The camera. Not vanity. Photographs of your life, which you will not take, and which you cannot retrospectively decide you wanted.
  • Two factor codes, parking apps, QR menus, work tools. The accumulated small dependencies that are invisible until they are not.

None of this makes it a bad idea. It makes it a real decision rather than a lifestyle aesthetic.

The aesthetic point is worth a sentence, because it affects how people make this choice. A dumb phone photographs well and signals something, and a decision that signals something is one you will defend past the point where it is working. If you find yourself wanting to tell people about it, notice that, and ask whether you would still want the device if nobody ever asked you about it.

Who a dumb phone genuinely suits

Three situations where I would say yes without much hesitation.

You have tried everything else and kept undoing it. Blockers, schedules, deleting apps, and you are back within a fortnight every time. At some point removing the capability is the honest answer.

Your use is closer to compulsive than habitual. If the phone is genuinely dominating your life rather than costing you an hour each evening, a half measure may not be enough.

You have a second device. Plenty of people carry a dumb phone and keep a tablet or laptop at home for banking and navigation. This gets you most of the benefit with far less friction, and it is what most successful switchers actually do rather than going fully without.

The cheaper test to run first

Before spending anything, simulate it for two weeks. If you cannot manage the simulation, the real thing will not work either.

Run it for a fortnight. If it holds, you have the benefit without the trade. If you reinstall things by day four, you have learned that the problem is the capability rather than the configuration, and the case for buying a different device is now evidence based rather than aspirational.

What the research suggests

The closest evidence is about removal rather than reduction, and it is encouraging for the dumb phone argument.

Allcott, Braghieri, Eichmeyer and Gentzkow randomly assigned a subset of 2,844 Facebook users to deactivate for four weeks, verified rather than self reported. They found small but significant increases in wellbeing, more offline socialising, reduced polarisation, and a large persistent drop in use after the experiment ended.1

Compare that with limiting. In a 2025 randomised trial where 58 students held under two hours a day for three weeks, screen time climbed back towards baseline by the six week follow up.2

Removal appears to stick. Limiting appears to pause. A dumb phone is the most complete version of removal available.

Be careful about how far to stretch that, though. Neither study tested a dumb phone. One tested deleting a single service for a month and the other tested a daily cap, so the inference that total hardware removal works even better is reasonable rather than demonstrated. Nobody has run the trial that would actually answer this question.

One honest caveat from the same deactivation study: it also found reduced factual news knowledge.1 Decide where your news comes from before you cut the route off.

The summary: it works, the cost is logistical rather than emotional, and the two week simulation tells you most of what buying one would.

Dumb phone: FAQs

Is a dumb phone worth it?

It works, because it removes the capability rather than asking you to resist it. Whether it is worth it depends on how much you rely on navigation, banking apps, tickets and group messaging.

What do you lose with a dumb phone?

Navigation, banking app authentication, app only tickets, group messaging, the camera, and the accumulated small dependencies like parking apps and two factor codes.

Is a dumb phone better than an app blocker?

It is a stronger intervention, because nothing can be undone in the moment. It is also a bigger trade. Try a stripped down smartphone for two weeks before deciding.

How do I test a dumb phone without buying one?

Delete every social, video and news app, delete or restrict your browser, turn off all non human notifications, and charge the phone in another room. That is most of the experience for free.

Do most people stick with a dumb phone?

Many end up with a hybrid, carrying a simple phone and keeping a tablet or laptop at home for banking and maps. That gets most of the benefit with much less disruption.

What is the downside nobody mentions?

The cost falls partly on other people. If your friends organise on a messaging app, you become the person who must be told separately, which is a real imposition worth acknowledging.
