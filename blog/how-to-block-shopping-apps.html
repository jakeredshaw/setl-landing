How to block shopping apps on iPhone

This is the one category where the time spent is not really the problem. The money is, and the fix is mostly not a blocker.

By Jacob Redshaw · 14 September 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer

To block shopping apps on iPhone, set a Screen Time Schedule covering the Shopping category and add the matching websites to Never Allow. More effective still is removing your stored card details and restricting in app purchases, because the real cost in this category is spending rather than hours.

You can block shopping apps on iPhone the same way as anything else, and for this category the block is the least important part of the fix.

Shopping apps do not usually cost you hours. They cost you money, late at night, in small amounts you cannot account for later. So the settings worth changing are the ones between you and a completed purchase.

Start with the money, not the app

Three changes, in order of how much they do.

Remove your stored card details. From every shopping app and from the browser. Having to go and find a physical card converts an impulse into a decision, and most late night purchases do not survive that. This is the single most effective thing on this page and it costs nothing.

Turn off one tap purchasing and saved addresses. The whole design of these apps is to compress the gap between wanting and buying. Reintroducing steps is the entire strategy.

Restrict in app purchases. Go to Settings, then Screen Time, then Content and Privacy Restrictions, and turn it on. Purchase restrictions live there.1

How to block shopping apps for set hours

Then add the block, for the hours when your judgement about value is worst.

  1. Settings, then Screen Time, then Screen Time Schedule.2
  2. Turn it on and cover the evening and night.
  3. Select the Shopping category rather than individual apps, so new ones are caught too.
  4. Add the websites as well, under Content and Privacy Restrictions, then Web Content, then Never Allow.1 Blocking the Amazon app and then buying through Safari is the usual way this fails.
  5. Strip your Always Allowed list, and remove any browser from it.

Why late night shopping specifically

The same conditions that make bedtime scrolling hard make bedtime spending easy, and they stack.

Your judgement is depleted, nothing is scheduled after it, and there is no immediate consequence. Add that the consequence is deferred by a month to a statement, and arrives detached from the moment of decision, and you have a behaviour with almost nothing holding it back.

Survey data suggests the hour itself is close to universally compromised. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine surveyed 2,007 US adults in 2025 and found most were prioritising screen time over sleep at bedtime.3

Worth being clear that I cannot point you to a trial measuring app blockers against impulse spending. Nobody has run it, as far as I can find, and anyone quoting a percentage for it is inventing one. The card removal advice is reasoning from how these interfaces are built rather than from a study.

A test worth running

Before blocking anything, go through the last three months of your statements and mark every purchase made after 9pm.

Two useful things come out of that. You find out whether this is actually a problem, rather than a feeling, and you find out which app is responsible. People are usually wrong about which one it is.

If the total is small, do nothing and spend your effort elsewhere. If it is not, you now have a number, which is far more motivating than a resolution.

The parcel is part of the loop

Worth naming, because it explains why this repeats even when the purchases are often unsatisfying.

The buying and the receiving are separated by days. You get a small reward when you order and a second one when a parcel arrives, by which point the decision is far enough away that you do not connect the two. Nothing in the sequence ever presents you with the cost.

Which is why reviewing your statements does more than any resolution. It is the only moment where the whole loop appears in one place. For the same reason, people who switch from saved cards to typing details each time usually report buying less without consciously deciding to, because the step they added back is the only step that was ever missing.

If the browsing is also costing you evenings, the general case is in how to use your phone less.

What about the browsing

Some people use shopping apps as a feed rather than to buy things, scrolling listings the way others scroll social media. If that is you, the time genuinely is the cost, and the scheduled block matters more than the payment settings.

A 2025 randomised controlled trial in BMC Medicine found that holding phone use under two hours a day for three weeks improved stress, wellbeing, depressive symptoms and sleep quality against a control group, with screen time returning towards baseline once the limit ended.4 As ever, permanent and modest beats strict and temporary.

Two settings outside Screen Time are worth the same ten minutes. Turn off marketing notifications from every shopping app, because a sale alert is a trigger you did not choose and it arrives precisely when a discount will look like a reason. And unsubscribe from the emails, since those route around any app block entirely by sending you to a browser.

Do this first: delete your saved cards from every shopping app and from your browser. It takes ten minutes and does more than any blocker.

Block shopping apps: FAQs

How do I block shopping apps on my iPhone?

Set a Screen Time Schedule covering the Shopping category, and add the matching websites to Never Allow under Content and Privacy Restrictions. Blocking the app alone leaves the website working.

What stops impulse buying better than a blocker?

Removing your stored card details from every shopping app and from your browser. Having to find a physical card converts an impulse into a decision, and most late night purchases do not survive that.

How do I stop in app purchases?

Settings, then Screen Time, then Content and Privacy Restrictions. Turn it on and restrict purchases there. It takes about thirty seconds and is worth doing before anything else.

Why do I shop late at night?

Your judgement is depleted, nothing is scheduled after it, and the consequence arrives a month later on a statement, detached from the moment of the decision.

Is there evidence that blocking apps reduces spending?

Not that I can find. No trial has measured app blockers against impulse spending, so treat any percentage you are quoted as invented. The card advice reasons from how the interfaces work.

How do I know if this is really a problem?

Go through three months of statements and mark every purchase made after 9pm. You will learn whether it is real and which app is responsible, and people are usually wrong about which.
