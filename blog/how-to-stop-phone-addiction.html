How to stop phone addiction

A four week plan that assumes you will have bad days, because every plan that assumes otherwise fails in the second week.

By Jacob Redshaw · 14 September 2026 · 7 min read

Quick answer

To stop phone addiction, change your environment rather than your intentions. Delete one app for two weeks, block fixed hours automatically, charge the phone outside the bedroom, and turn off notifications from companies. Expect it to take weeks and plan for bad days.

Most plans to stop phone addiction fail in week two, and they all fail the same way. They assume a version of you who stays motivated. That person does not exist past about day nine.

What follows assumes you will have bad days, lose your temper with it, and reinstall something. It is built to survive that.

One note on the word first. Phone addiction is not a recognised diagnosis in ICD-11 or the DSM-5. What the World Health Organization does recognise is gaming disorder, defined by impaired control, gaming taking priority over other activities, and continuing despite negative consequences, normally evident for at least 12 months and significantly impairing your functioning.1 If that describes you, go past this page and see a doctor. If it does not, you still have a problem worth fixing, and you do not need a label to fix it.

Why willpower is the wrong tool

Phone use is not one decision. It is roughly one per second for as long as the phone is in your hand, and you must win every single one. A 99 percent success rate across an hour still ends with you scrolling, because one loss is enough and then the counter resets.

So anything shaped like trying harder is asking to win an unlimited run of coin flips. Everything below instead changes the situation so the flips stop happening.

The four week plan to stop phone addiction

Week one: measure and remove one thing

  1. Open Settings, then Screen Time, and write down your real weekly average. Use the number on the screen, not the one you would say out loud.
  2. Delete your single worst app. Not all of them. One. For two weeks, not forever, because forever is too big to agree to and that is why you keep postponing it.
  3. Turn off every notification that is not from a person. Keep calls and messages from humans. Kill everything sent by a company to bring you back.

Change nothing else. People fail here by doing all four weeks at once.

Week two: fix the night

  1. Charge the phone in another room. Buy a cheap alarm clock. This costs about ten pounds and does more than any software on this page.
  2. Set a Screen Time Schedule from 30 minutes before you want to be asleep until you want to get up. It is free and already on your iPhone.
  3. Empty your Always Allowed list down to Phone and Messages. Anything left in there ignores the schedule entirely, which is why most people think the feature is broken.

Week three: fix the mornings and the gaps

  1. Extend the block to cover your first waking hour.
  2. Move remaining apps off your first home screen so you have to search for them. Typing the name turns a reflex into a decision.
  3. Put something in the gaps. You reach for it in queues, adverts, and the ten minutes before sleep. If nothing fills those, you go back. A book by the bed is enough and it does not need to be a good book.

Week four: decide what is permanent

  1. Check the number again and compare it with week one.
  2. Decide what stays forever. Probably the charger move and the night block.
  3. Reinstall the deleted app only if you genuinely missed it. Most people find they did not, which is information you could not have got any other way.

What the evidence says to expect

Be realistic rather than hopeful. A 2025 randomised controlled trial in BMC Medicine took 111 students averaging 276 minutes a day and had 58 hold under two hours a day for three weeks. Against the control group they improved on stress, wellbeing, depressive symptoms and sleep quality, with small to medium effect sizes.2

The same trial found screen time returning towards baseline once the limit stopped.2 That is why week four is about deciding what becomes permanent rather than celebrating.

There is better news on deletion specifically. A randomised study of 2,844 Facebook users found that four weeks of verified deactivation produced small but significant increases in wellbeing, more offline socialising, and a large persistent drop in use after the experiment had finished.3 The same study also found reduced factual news knowledge, so choose a deliberate news source before you delete a feed.

When to stop reading this and see a doctor

Two signs, and they are both worth taking seriously.

First, if the phone use is a way of managing low mood, anxiety or loneliness. Then the phone is the coping mechanism rather than the problem, and removing it without addressing what sits underneath usually produces a worse week rather than a better one.

Second, if it is genuinely impairing your work, sleep or relationships and has been for a long time. Depression, anxiety and ADHD all commonly sit underneath this pattern, all produce exactly these symptoms, and all are treatable.

If you only do two things: charge your phone in another room, and block your chosen apps automatically from 30 minutes before bedtime. Together they cost about ten pounds and address the hours that cost you most.

Stop phone addiction: FAQs

How long does it take to stop phone addiction?

Expect weeks rather than days, and plan for it to be permanent rather than a fixed period. In one trial, usage returned towards baseline within six weeks of the limit ending, so the arrangement has to stay.

What is the first thing I should do?

Delete your single worst app for two weeks and turn off every notification that is not from a person. Doing one thing properly beats attempting six and abandoning all of them.

Can you stop phone addiction with willpower alone?

Unlikely, because it is not one decision but thousands, and you have to win every one. Changing your environment removes the decisions instead of asking you to keep winning them.

Should I delete social media completely?

If you can, the evidence for deletion is better than for limiting. A randomised study found four weeks of deactivation produced lasting reductions in use, plus small wellbeing gains.

What if I relapse and reinstall everything?

Expected, and not a failure. The plan above assumes bad days. Keep the parts that are environmental, like the charger in another room, because those keep working when your motivation does not.

When should I see a doctor about phone use?

If you use the phone to manage low mood, anxiety or loneliness, or if it has been impairing your work, sleep or relationships for a long time. Depression, anxiety and ADHD all produce this pattern and are treatable.
