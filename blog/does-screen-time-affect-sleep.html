Does screen time affect sleep?

Blue light gets all the attention and is the smaller half of the problem. The bigger half is much more boring and much easier to fix.

By Jacob Redshaw · 14 September 2026 · 7 min read

Quick answer

Yes. Screen time affect sleep claims hold up, through two routes: light suppressing melatonin and shifting your body clock, and simply staying up later than you intended. The second route is the bigger effect for most people, and it is the one worth fixing first.

Ask whether screen time affect sleep is a real phenomenon and you will get a confident yes built almost entirely on one study about blue light, usually misquoted.

The honest version has two parts, and the part nobody talks about is the bigger one.

Route one: light, melatonin and your body clock

The study everybody is thinking of is Chang, Aeschbach, Duffy and Czeisler, published in PNAS in 2015.1

What they found is real. Compared with reading a printed book, reading a light emitting eReader before bed produced reduced melatonin secretion, a circadian delay of more than an hour, longer to fall asleep, reduced evening sleepiness, reduced REM sleep and worse next morning alertness.1

That is a serious set of findings. Now the context that gets removed whenever it is quoted.

  • It was 12 adults. Twelve. A careful inpatient laboratory study, but very small.
  • They read for four hours. From 6pm to 10pm, every evening. That is not twenty minutes of scrolling in bed.
  • It was a lab, not a bedroom. There is a published critique arguing the lighting conditions may have overstated real world effects, with a reply from the authors.2

So blue light does something. It is probably not doing as much to you as four hours of iPad in a sleep lab did to those twelve people. Anyone citing this study to sell you glasses is stretching it well past what it showed.

Route two: you stay up later

This is the bigger effect and it is almost embarrassingly simple. You meant to sleep at 11. It is now 12:40. Nothing to do with wavelengths.

No amount of filtering changes this. Night Shift, dark mode and amber glasses all address the smaller mechanism and none of them address the one actually costing you ninety minutes.

The behaviour is close to universal. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine surveyed 2,007 US adults in 2025 and found most were prioritising screen time over sleep at bedtime.3

And it shows up in interventions. A 2025 randomised controlled trial in BMC Medicine took 111 students averaging 276 minutes of phone a day, with 58 holding under two hours a day for three weeks. Sleep quality on the Insomnia Severity Index improved against the control group, with a mean difference of 2.59.4

Note what that intervention was. Not a blue light filter. Not reading on paper. Just less phone.

Because of what feeds do to endings.

A book has chapters. An episode finishes. Each gives you a moment where continuing becomes a choice, and in that moment you usually choose correctly and go to bed.

A feed has no end, so that moment never arrives. You do not decide to stay up. You simply never reach the point where you would have decided to stop. That is a design property, not a weakness in you.

Then two things make it worse at exactly that hour. Your judgement is depleted, and nothing is scheduled after it, so there is no external force ending the session.

There is a third factor that rarely gets named. Lying in the dark without a phone means being alone with whatever you have been avoiding thinking about all day, and for a lot of people the scrolling is partly there to prevent exactly that. If this is what is happening, no blocker solves it on its own, and the honest route is dealing with the thing you are avoiding rather than treating the symptom as a discipline failure.

What to do, in order of what works

  1. Fix the timing, not the colour. Make the apps unavailable at a fixed hour. This addresses the bigger mechanism and it is free on an iPhone through a Screen Time Schedule.
  2. Charge the phone in another room. About ten pounds for an alarm clock, and it beats every software setting here.
  3. Start the block before you are in bed. Thirty minutes before you want to be asleep. Once you are lying down holding it, you have already lost.
  4. Then use Night Shift if you like. It is free and it probably helps a little. Just do not let it be your whole plan.
The one line version: blue light is a minor effect wearing a lab coat. Going to bed ninety minutes late is the thing actually costing you sleep.

What this does not mean

It does not mean screens are harmless. Both mechanisms are real and they add together.

It also does not mean fixing your phone fixes your sleep. If you have persistent insomnia, wake repeatedly, or feel exhausted after a full night, that is worth seeing a doctor about rather than solving with an app. Phone use at bedtime is one input among several, and it is simply the easiest one to change.

And be careful with causation. Most of the data linking screens to worse sleep is observational, which means people who sleep badly may also use phones more, rather than the other way round. The randomised trials are where the stronger evidence sits, and they are smaller and fewer than the headlines suggest.

Screen time affect sleep: FAQs

Does screen time really affect sleep?

Yes, through two routes. Light can suppress melatonin and delay your body clock, and screens keep you up later than you intended. The second effect is larger for most people and easier to fix.

Is blue light before bed actually bad?

It has a measurable effect, but the famous study used 12 adults reading an eReader for four hours in a sleep lab. That is far from twenty minutes of scrolling, so treat the finding as real but modest.

Do blue light glasses help you sleep?

They target the smaller of the two mechanisms. Even if they work perfectly, they do nothing about going to bed ninety minutes later than you meant to, which is the bigger problem.

Does Night Shift or dark mode improve sleep?

Possibly a little. Both are free so there is no reason not to use them, but neither addresses the timing problem, so do not rely on them as your only change.

How long before bed should I stop using my phone?

Thirty minutes before you want to be asleep is a practical target, mainly because stopping is much harder once you are already lying down holding the phone.

Does fixing my phone use fix my sleep?

Not on its own. Phone use is one input among several. Persistent insomnia, repeated waking or exhaustion after a full night are worth taking to a doctor rather than treating with an app.
