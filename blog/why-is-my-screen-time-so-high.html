Screen Time

Why is my screen time so high?

Before changing anything, work out whether the number is real. Some of it is, and some of it is counting things you were not doing.

By Jacob Redshaw · 14 September 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer

If your screen time so high it looks implausible, passive activity is usually the cause: music, podcasts, navigation and video playing to a television all get counted. After discounting those, look at your pickups and notification counts rather than the total hours figure.

Finding out your screen time so high that it seems implausible is a common experience, and the first useful step is working out how much of the number is genuine.

Some of it is. Some of it is counting things you were not really doing.

First, check what is inflating it

Four things commonly make the figure look worse than your actual phone use.

Music and podcasts. If an app is playing while you drive, cook or train, that time can appear in the report. You were not looking at the screen for ninety minutes, you were listening.

Navigation. A long drive with Maps open is logged, even though the phone was on a mount and you were watching the road.

Video playing to a TV. Streaming from your phone to a television, or leaving something running, counts.

Other devices. If you share Screen Time across an iPad or a Mac, the headline figure may be combining them rather than reporting your phone alone.

Go to Settings, then Screen Time, and look at the breakdown by app and category rather than the total. If the top entries are music, maps or video, your real problem is smaller than the number suggests.

If your screen time so high looks wrong, check pickups instead

Not total hours. Pickups.

The same screen shows how many times you picked the phone up, and which app you opened first each time. That figure tells you far more than the total, because the shape of the problem is usually hundreds of short unremembered visits rather than a few long sessions.

Two people with four hours a day can have completely different problems. One watched two films. The other picked the phone up two hundred times and remembers none of it. The second person is the one for whom the time has genuinely disappeared.

Ask yourself this honestly: can you account for yesterday's screen time hour by hour. If you can, the hours were chosen and you may be fine with them. If you cannot, they were not chosen, and that is the actual complaint.

Most sessions are triggered rather than chosen. A notification arrives and you respond. Check how many notifications you receive daily on the same Screen Time screen. For most people it is several hundred, and almost all of them are sent by companies rather than people.

Feeds have no stopping point. A book has chapters and an episode ends, and each gives you a moment where continuing becomes a choice. A feed never produces that moment, so a two minute check becomes twenty.

You reach for it in gaps. Queues, adverts, the pause between tasks, the ten minutes before sleep. None of those feel like decisions, which is why the time is invisible afterwards.

What a high number does not mean

Be careful with the guilt here, because the subject is full of invented thresholds.

There is no evidence based number above which screen time becomes harmful. Anyone quoting one is guessing. Two hours of messaging your friends and two hours of doomscrolling are not the same thing, and the report cannot tell them apart.

What has been measured is what happens when people cut back. A 2025 randomised controlled trial in BMC Medicine took 111 students averaging 276 minutes a day and had 58 hold under two hours a day for three weeks. Against the control group they improved on stress, wellbeing, depressive symptoms and sleep quality, with small to medium effect sizes.1

Small to medium. Worth having, not a transformation. And screen time climbed back towards baseline once the limit ended,1 which is the part that tells you to plan something permanent rather than a dramatic week.

It is also worth knowing that comparing your number to other people's is close to useless. Reported averages vary enormously depending on who was surveyed, how, and whether passive time was counted, so the figure you saw quoted somewhere may not be measuring the same thing your phone is measuring. The only comparison that tells you anything is your number against your own number last month.

What to do with the information

  1. Discount the passive time. Music, maps and video playing to a TV are not the problem. Subtract them mentally before you panic.
  2. Target the first app of each pickup. Screen Time tells you which app you open first most often. That app is your actual habit, whatever the totals say.
  3. Turn off every notification not sent by a person. This attacks triggered sessions, which are most of them.
  4. Pick one hour, not the whole day. The hour before bed repeats 365 times a year and has the least defending it.
  5. Take 20 percent off, not 80. Five hours to four is 365 hours a year and you will actually keep it.
Two minutes: open Settings, then Screen Time, and write down three numbers. Total, pickups, and notifications. The second two will tell you more than the first.

Screen time so high: FAQs

Why is my screen time so high when I barely use my phone?

Usually because passive activity is being counted. Music, podcasts, navigation and video playing to a television all log time even though you were not looking at the screen.

Does Screen Time count music and podcasts?

It can, which is why the total often looks implausible. Check the breakdown by app and category rather than the headline figure, and discount anything you were listening to rather than watching.

What is a normal amount of screen time?

There is no evidence based threshold, and anyone quoting one precisely is guessing. Two hours of messaging friends and two hours of doomscrolling are not comparable, and the report cannot distinguish them.

Is pickups a better number than total hours?

Usually yes. Hundreds of short unremembered pickups is a different problem from two long sessions, and the pickup count plus the first app opened tells you where your actual habit is.

Why do I pick up my phone so many times a day?

Most sessions are triggered rather than chosen. Check your daily notification count on the same screen, then turn off everything sent by a company rather than a person.

Should I be worried about a high number?

Only if you cannot account for where it went. Chosen hours are fine. The complaint worth acting on is time you have no memory of spending.
