The phone free weekend

Two days is long enough to notice something and short enough to agree to. Here is how to do one without it being a performance.

By Jacob Redshaw · 14 September 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer

A phone free weekend means your chosen apps are unavailable from Friday evening to Monday morning, with calls and maps still working. It is a useful experiment rather than a fix, because benefits fade once usage returns, so treat it as information gathering.

A phone free weekend is the most agreeable version of this whole subject. Two days is short enough that you will actually do it and long enough to tell you something you did not know.

It is also not a fix, and being clear about that up front is the difference between a useful experiment and another abandoned resolution.

Why it is not a fix

A 2025 randomised controlled trial in BMC Medicine took 111 students averaging 276 minutes of phone a day and had 58 hold under two hours a day for three weeks. Against the control group they improved on stress, wellbeing, depressive symptoms and sleep quality, with small to medium effect sizes.1

Then screen time climbed back quickly, and by the six week follow up it was approaching baseline.1

That was three weeks. A weekend is two days. Whatever you feel on Sunday evening will not survive the week unless you change something permanent, and expecting otherwise is how people end up concluding that nothing works.

So do it for information rather than transformation. The question you are answering is which parts you actually miss.

How to do a phone free weekend

  1. Keep calls, maps and payments. Going fully without is a different and much harder experiment, and the logistics will become the story. Block the apps, not the device.
  2. Set it up on Thursday. Use a Screen Time Schedule running Friday evening to Monday morning, and have someone else set the passcode if you have one handy.
  3. Strip your Always Allowed list. Otherwise the whole thing does nothing.
  4. Block the websites too. Over two days you will certainly try the browser version, probably on Saturday afternoon, and you will not feel like you are cheating.
  5. Tell two people. Not as an announcement. Just so nobody thinks you are ignoring them.
  6. Plan Saturday morning. This is the step people skip and it decides the whole thing. An empty weekend is filled by the phone whatever your settings say.

What to expect, roughly

Friday evening is the hardest part and it is mostly about the hand reaching rather than wanting anything specific. You will pick the phone up repeatedly and put it down again, and that is the single most informative thing that will happen, because it shows you how much of the habit has nothing to do with content.

Saturday tends to feel long in a good way. Sunday usually feels normal, and the normality is the finding.

Boredom on Saturday afternoon is not a failure of the experiment. It is the experiment. Boredom is the state your phone has been preventing, and sitting in it for twenty minutes without fixing it is the thing most people have not done for years.

What the related evidence shows

The closest study to a deliberate break is about deactivation. Allcott, Braghieri, Eichmeyer and Gentzkow randomly assigned a subset of 2,844 Facebook users to deactivate for four weeks, verified rather than self reported. They found small but significant wellbeing increases, more offline socialising including with family and friends, reduced polarisation, and a large persistent drop in use after the study ended.2

Two caveats worth carrying. The freed time went partly into watching television alone,2 so plan your weekend rather than assuming it fills itself well. And the same study found reduced factual news knowledge,2 which over a single weekend does not matter at all.

Who should not bother

Two cases where a weekend is the wrong idea.

If you have already done several and nothing changed, stop doing them. Repeating an experiment that keeps producing the same result is not persistence, and the pattern tells you what you needed to know: the problem is not a lack of time away, it is that nothing is in place during the week. Skip to the permanent change.

And if you are using it to feel better about the rest of your habits, it will work for about a day and then make things slightly worse, because a weekend of abstinence tends to license the following week. That effect is familiar from dieting and it operates the same way here.

What to do on Monday

This is the part that determines whether the weekend was worth anything.

Write down, on Sunday night, which apps you genuinely missed and which you did not think about. Most people are surprised by at least one in each direction.

Then keep one small permanent change rather than planning another weekend. The bedtime block is the usual candidate, because it repeats 365 times a year and costs almost nothing. One permanent hour beats four heroic weekends a year, which is the whole lesson of the trial above.

And do not post about it. A weekend announced on the way in becomes a thing you are seen to be doing, which changes what it is for, and the ending gets reported as a success regardless of what actually happened. The useful version is private and slightly dull, and the only person who needs the result is you.

Set it up Thursday: apps and their websites blocked Friday 18:00 to Monday 07:00, calls and maps left alone, and something planned for Saturday morning.

Phone free weekend: FAQs

How do I do a phone free weekend?

Block your chosen apps and their websites from Friday evening to Monday morning while keeping calls, maps and payments working. Set it up on Thursday and plan Saturday morning.

Should I give up my phone completely for the weekend?

Not the first time. Going fully without turns the logistics into the story. Blocking the apps rather than the device keeps the experiment about the habit.

Will a phone free weekend fix my phone use?

No. In one trial, three weeks of reduced use returned towards baseline within six weeks of the limit ending. Treat a weekend as information gathering, not a cure.

What is the hardest part?

Friday evening, and mostly the hand reaching rather than wanting anything specific. That is also the most informative part, because it shows how little of the habit is about content.

What should I do with the time?

Decide before Friday. An empty weekend gets filled by the phone whatever your settings say, and in one study freed time went partly into watching television alone.

What should I do on Monday?

Write down which apps you genuinely missed, then keep one small permanent change rather than planning another weekend. A nightly block beats four heroic weekends a year.
