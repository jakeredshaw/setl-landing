How to block apps while driving

This is the one case where the right tool is not a blocker at all, and where the stakes are not your evening.

By Jacob Redshaw · 14 September 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer

To block apps while driving, use Driving Focus in Settings rather than a Screen Time schedule. Driving Focus activates automatically when it detects you are driving, silences notifications and can auto reply to messages. A time based schedule cannot know when you are in a car.

If you want to block apps while driving, the tool you want is not a blocker. It is Driving Focus, and the reason is simple: a Screen Time schedule works on the clock, and a clock does not know when you are in a car.

This is also the one topic on this site where the stakes are not your evening or your sleep. So I am going to be direct rather than balanced.

How to block apps while driving: use Driving Focus

Driving Focus lives in Settings, then Focus, then Driving. The important part is the activation setting.

  1. Open Settings and tap Focus.
  2. Tap Driving.
  3. Set it to activate automatically, so it detects driving rather than waiting for you to turn it on. This is the whole thing. A mode you have to enable is a mode you will not enable on the journey it mattered.
  4. Turn on the auto reply option so people messaging you are told you are driving. It removes the sense that someone is waiting, which is most of the pull.
  5. Allow calls from whoever genuinely needs to reach you, and nobody else.

If your car supports it, pairing by Bluetooth or CarPlay makes the detection more reliable than motion alone.

Apple's wider Screen Time documentation is the reference for the rest of the blocking features on your phone,1 but Focus is a separate system and it is the one that matters here. The distinction confuses people who go looking for driving options inside Screen Time and cannot find any.

One more setting worth changing while you are in there. Turn off notification previews on the Lock Screen, or at least for messaging apps. A preview is the thing that converts a glance into a pickup, because reading half a message creates an obligation to read the rest.

Why a Screen Time schedule is the wrong tool

Screen Time blocks by the clock. You could block everything from 08:00 to 09:00 because that is usually your commute, and it would be wrong on the day you drive at three in the afternoon, and wrong again on the day you are on a train and would like to read something.

Driving is a context rather than a time, and only a context aware tool handles it. Nothing built on Screen Time, including any third party blocker, can tell whether you are in a car.

So this is a case where the blockers in this category, mine included, are not the answer. Use Apple's.

The setting that matters more than any blocking

Put the phone somewhere you cannot reach it.

Glovebox, back seat, in a bag. Not in a cradle on the windscreen, not in the cupholder, not face down on the passenger seat.

Every software approach still leaves the device within arm's reach, and the reach is the behaviour. A notification you cannot see on a phone you cannot reach is categorically different from a silenced phone you can pick up at a red light.

If you need navigation, set the route before you move and then do not touch it again. A mounted phone running maps is fine. A mounted phone is also a mounted phone, which is the uncomfortable part.

Why the pull is so strong in a car

Three things, worth naming because knowing them helps.

Driving is often boring. Particularly a familiar route, where attention has very little to do and goes looking for something. That is precisely the state in which people reach for a phone.

Red lights feel like permission. Stationary feels like not driving. It is not, and the pickup rarely ends when the light changes.

You have done it before without consequence. Which is the worst possible training data, because the absence of a crash gets learned as safety rather than as luck.

What the evidence here is

I am not going to cite the phone reduction trials on this page, because they are about wellbeing and sleep and have nothing to say about driving.

What I will say plainly is that this is not a productivity or lifestyle question. The reason to deal with it is not that your screen time figure looks bad. If you want the context on why reaching for a phone is so automatic generally, that is in how to focus without your phone, and the mechanism is the same: the reach arrives in gaps of attention, and a dull stretch of road is a gap of attention.

If you drive for work, or drive other people's children, it is worth treating this as a policy rather than a preference. Decide once that the phone lives in the glovebox on every journey, and you never have to make the judgement again on a specific trip where you are late and expecting a message. That is the same principle as every other arrangement on this site, which is that a decision made in advance by a calm person beats one made in the moment by a distracted one. It is just that here the distracted person is driving.

Two minutes: Settings, Focus, Driving, set activation to automatic and turn on auto reply. Then put the phone out of reach before you start the engine.

Block apps while driving: FAQs

How do I block apps while driving on iPhone?

Use Driving Focus in Settings, then Focus, then Driving, and set it to activate automatically. A Screen Time schedule works on the clock and cannot tell when you are in a car.

Why not use a Screen Time schedule for driving?

Because driving is a context rather than a time. A clock based block will be wrong whenever you drive outside your usual hours, and wrong again when you are on a train.

Does Driving Focus turn on by itself?

It can, and you should set it to. A mode you have to remember to enable is one you will not enable on the journey where it mattered.

What is the single most effective change?

Putting the phone physically out of reach before you start the engine. Software still leaves the device within arm's reach, and the reach is the behaviour.

Is it safe to use my phone at a red light?

Being stationary feels like not driving and is not, and the pickup rarely ends when the light changes. Treat the whole journey as one continuous period.

Can I still use maps?

Yes. Set the route before you move and then do not touch it. A mounted phone running navigation is reasonable, but it is still a phone within reach.
