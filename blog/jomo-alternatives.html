Jomo alternatives

Jomo is the most configurable blocker on iPhone. Most people searching for an alternative want the opposite of that.

By Jacob Redshaw · 14 September 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer

Most people looking for Jomo alternatives want less configuration rather than more. one sec is the simplest, Apple Screen Time is free and enforced at system level, Opal is the most established paid option, and a scheduled blocker suits a specific problem hour.

Searches for jomo alternatives usually come from one of two places, and it is worth knowing which one you are in before choosing anything.

Either Jomo was too much, or Jomo was not quite the right shape. Those lead to different answers.

For context: on the UK App Store on 2 November 2026, Jomo held 4.8 stars from 471 ratings and was free to download.1 A strong rating on a smaller review base than the biggest names in the category.

The problem with the most capable tool

Jomo goes deep on routines, schedules and rules. If you enjoy building a system, it will let you build a very good one.

The failure mode is specific and common: you start configuring, it is genuinely interesting, you get most of the way through, and then you never finish. A blocker you have not finished setting up blocks nothing. Meanwhile you feel like you have addressed the problem, because you spent an hour on it.

There is a second version of the same trap. You do finish, the system is elaborate, and then your life changes slightly and the whole arrangement needs rework. The more precisely a setup fits your circumstances, the more fragile it is when circumstances move.

If either of those sounds familiar, the answer is fewer decisions rather than better ones.

This runs against the instinct, which is that a problem you have failed to solve needs a more powerful tool. In this category the opposite usually applies. Every setting is a small ongoing tax: something to maintain, something to reconsider, and something to adjust downwards on the night you least should. A setup with one decision in it has one point of failure. A setup with thirty has thirty, and you will not notice which one went.

There is also a simple test for whether a tool is too elaborate for you. If you cannot describe your current setup out loud in two sentences, you do not really know what it does, which means you cannot tell whether it is working or quietly broken.

Jomo alternatives, from simplest upwards

Simplest: one sec

Almost nothing to configure. It intercepts the app you opened, makes you pause, then asks whether you still want it. On 2 November 2026 it was free to download with 4.8 stars from 7,133 UK ratings, from riedel.wtf apps.2

It is a friction tool rather than a blocker, so it will not take an app away. For reflexive opening, that is often all that is needed.

Free and enforced: Apple Screen Time

Already installed, and it has one advantage no third party app can match: the controls live in Settings behind a passcode rather than inside the blocking app, so you can hand the passcode to somebody else and genuinely separate yourself from the dials.

Set a Screen Time Schedule, strip your Always Allowed list to Phone and Messages, and use a passcode you have not memorised. For many people that is the whole solution.

Middle ground, free: ScreenZen

Pauses, intention prompts and opening limits. Free to download on 2 November 2026 with 4.8 stars from 7,588 UK ratings.3 Less setup than Jomo, more than one sec.

Most established paid option: Opal

Opal: Screen Time Control from Opal OS Corporation held 4.6 stars from 14,776 UK ratings on 2 November 2026, by far the largest review base in the category.4 Sessions, schedules and a harder focus mode, with sensible defaults so you are not obliged to build anything. The safest buy and the most expensive mainstream choice.

If the problem is one specific hour: SETL

Mine, so discount it accordingly. You set a bedtime once and your chosen apps go automatically every night, with no routines to design. Ending a block early is a deliberate press and hold rather than a tap. It launched in late October 2026 and has no reviews yet, so if a track record matters, Opal is the answer.

The thing to check before switching at all

Switching apps is often a way of feeling productive about a problem without changing anything. The new tool has a novelty period, works for ten days, and then you are back where you were with a different icon on your home screen.

Worth bearing in mind what the evidence says about durability. A 2025 randomised controlled trial in BMC Medicine found genuine improvements in stress, wellbeing, depressive symptoms and sleep when 58 students held under two hours a day for three weeks, and then found screen time climbing back towards baseline once the arrangement ended.5

So the question is not which app has the best features. It is which arrangement you will still be running in six months, and that usually comes down to how few decisions it asks of you.

Jomo alternatives: FAQs

What is the best alternative to Jomo?

Usually something simpler. one sec has almost nothing to configure, Apple Screen Time is free and enforced at system level, and Opal offers similar depth with more polish and sensible defaults.

Why is Jomo hard to stick with?

Because its strength is configurability. People commonly start building a setup, find it genuinely interesting, and never finish, and an unfinished blocker blocks nothing.

Is there a simpler app blocker than Jomo?

Yes. one sec is the simplest of the well rated options, with a pause rather than a rules engine. Apple's own Screen Time Schedule is also a single setting once you have chosen the hours.

Is Jomo free?

It was free to download on the UK App Store in November 2026, with 4.8 stars from 471 ratings. As with any free app in this category, read the privacy label before installing.

Jomo or Opal?

Jomo if you want more control and enjoy configuring things. Opal if you want strong defaults and the reassurance of a large review base, since it had 14,776 UK ratings in November 2026.

Will switching blockers actually help?

Only if the new one asks fewer decisions of you. Changing apps often feels like progress while changing nothing, because every tool has a novelty period of about ten days.
