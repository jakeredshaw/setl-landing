What is doomscrolling?

A definition, the mechanism underneath it, and why it peaks at the exact hour you are least able to stop.

By Jacob Redshaw · 14 September 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer

Doomscrolling is the compulsive consumption of negative news and social media content, continued past the point where you know it is making you feel worse. The word combines doom with scrolling and spread widely from 2020. It is distinct from ordinary scrolling because the content is specifically distressing.

Doomscrolling is the compulsive consumption of bad news, continued past the point where you are aware it is making you feel worse. The word is a compound of doom and scrolling, and it entered general use around 2020.

What separates it from ordinary scrolling is the content. Ordinary scrolling is mostly neutral and forgettable. Doomscrolling is specifically distressing, and the distress is not a side effect. It is the thing holding your attention.

Why bad news specifically

Three mechanisms stack up, and none of them are a flaw in you.

Threat gets priority

Attention is weighted towards possible danger, which is a sensible default for an animal that needs to notice predators. Applied to an infinite feed of global events, the same default means the alarming item always wins over the pleasant one.

Uncertainty feels solvable

This is the part that makes doomscrolling feel purposeful rather than self destructive. You are not reading bad news for fun. You are trying to find the piece of information that makes the situation make sense, or feel contained.

That piece does not exist, which is why you keep going. The search feels rational right up until you notice it has been forty minutes and you know less than when you started, because you have now read nine contradictory accounts.

The feed has no end

A newspaper runs out. A bulletin finishes. Each gives you a moment where continuing becomes an active choice, and at that moment most people stop.

A feed never produces that moment. So you do not choose to keep reading bad news. You simply never arrive at the point where you would have chosen to stop. This is the single biggest factor and it is architectural rather than psychological.

Why doomscrolling is worst at night

Four things line up at bedtime, all pushing the same direction.

  1. Your judgement is spent. Whatever capacity you had for interrupting yourself went on the day.
  2. Nothing is scheduled after it. At 2pm something comes next and ends the session for you. At 11pm nothing does.
  3. The alternative is being alone with your thoughts. Which is, for a lot of people, exactly what the scrolling is there to prevent. Bad news about the world is more tolerable than quiet about your own life.
  4. Nobody is watching. No consequence arrives until morning.

This is not a rare affliction. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine surveyed 2,007 US adults in 2025 and found most were choosing screen time over sleep at bedtime.1

What doomscrolling costs

Be careful with the claims here, because this is where the subject usually goes wrong. There is no good trial isolating doomscrolling specifically and measuring what it does to you. Anyone giving you a figure for it is guessing.

What has been measured is general phone reduction. A 2025 randomised controlled trial in BMC Medicine took 111 students averaging 276 minutes a day and had 58 hold under two hours a day for three weeks. Against the control group they improved on stress, wellbeing, depressive symptoms and sleep quality, with small to medium effect sizes.2

There is also a finding that complicates the obvious advice. A randomised study of 2,844 Facebook users found that four weeks of verified deactivation produced small wellbeing gains, more offline socialising and less political polarisation, but also reduced factual news knowledge.3

So the honest position is not that following the news is bad for you. It is that an endless feed is a poor delivery mechanism for it.

That distinction is worth holding on to, because the usual advice collapses it. Told that doomscrolling is harmful, people either ignore the advice entirely or swing to avoiding news altogether, and the second option has its own cost. Being uninformed is not a form of wellbeing. What the evidence points to is something duller and more workable: the same interest in the world, routed through something that finishes.

It also explains why the feeling of doomscrolling is so specific. Reading a newspaper about a crisis can be sobering but it tends to leave you feeling informed. Forty minutes in a feed about the same crisis tends to leave you agitated and no clearer, because a feed is optimised for the next item rather than for a complete account. You are not being given a picture. You are being given an unending supply of fragments, each of which implies a picture you never reach.

What to do about it

  1. Change the delivery, not the interest. Pick a news source with an end: a newspaper, a daily newsletter, a radio bulletin. You get informed and you get a finishing point.
  2. Remove it at specific hours rather than permanently. The hour before bed is the cheapest to give up and the one doing most of the damage.
  3. Notice the question you are trying to answer. If you can name it, you can usually tell within a minute whether more scrolling will answer it. It will not.
  4. Put the phone in another room at night. About ten pounds for an alarm clock. The most reliable intervention here and it requires nothing of you once done.
The distinction worth keeping: staying informed is a decision with an end. Doomscrolling is the same impulse delivered through a mechanism designed not to have one.

Doomscrolling: FAQs

What does doomscrolling mean?

Compulsively scrolling through negative news and social media content, past the point where you know it is making you feel worse. The word combines doom and scrolling and spread widely from around 2020.

Why do I doomscroll if it makes me feel bad?

Because it feels like problem solving. You are searching for the piece of information that will make the situation make sense. That piece does not exist, so the search has no natural end.

Is doomscrolling bad for you?

Probably, though no trial has isolated it specifically, so be sceptical of anyone quoting a number. General phone reduction has measured benefits for stress, mood and sleep.

Why is doomscrolling worse at night?

Your judgement is depleted, nothing is scheduled after it to end the session, the alternative is lying alone with your thoughts, and there is no consequence until morning.

Should I stop following the news?

Not necessarily. One randomised study found that people who deactivated Facebook knew less about current events. Better to change the delivery to something with an ending, like a newspaper or a daily newsletter.

What is the difference between doomscrolling and normal scrolling?

The content. Ordinary scrolling is mostly neutral and forgettable. Doomscrolling is specifically distressing, and the distress is part of what holds your attention rather than a side effect.
