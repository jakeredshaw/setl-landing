How to read more instead of scrolling

Reading does not lose to the phone because people prefer phones. It loses on availability, and availability is fixable.

By Jacob Redshaw · 14 September 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer

To read more instead of scrolling, make the book more available than the phone rather than relying on preference. Put the phone in another room, keep a book within arm's reach of the bed, and block your apps at a fixed hour so the choice is already made.

Everyone who wants to read more instead of scrolling frames it as a willpower problem, and it is not. It is an availability problem, and that is much easier to solve.

Why reading loses

Not because people prefer phones. Because of three practical asymmetries.

The phone is closer. It is in your hand. The book is on a shelf in another room. At the moment of choosing, distance decides almost everything, and no stated preference survives a thirty second walk.

Reading has a starting cost. The first two pages are work. You have to rebuild where you were, re enter the voice, concentrate. Scrolling has no starting cost at all, which means on a tired evening it wins before any comparison of value happens.

Scrolling is adjustable to your state. Too tired to read is a real condition. Too tired to scroll does not exist. So as the evening wears on, the phone becomes relatively more attractive at exactly the hour you had planned to read.

None of those are about taste. All three are fixable by changing where things are.

How to read more instead of scrolling, practically

  1. Put the phone in another room and the book where the phone was. This single swap does most of the work. You are not relying on choosing the book, you are relying on it being the only thing there.
  2. Buy a cheap alarm clock. About ten pounds. If the phone is your alarm, it stays in the bedroom and the swap is impossible.
  3. Block your apps at a fixed hour. Thirty minutes before you want to be asleep, automatically. A free Screen Time Schedule does this. Then the choice has been made in advance by someone competent.
  4. Lower the bar absurdly. One page. Not thirty minutes, not a chapter. One page is small enough that you cannot reasonably refuse, and it gets you past the starting cost, which is where the resistance lives.
  5. Read things you actually enjoy. This is the most commonly ignored advice in the genre. If you are three weeks into a book you are not enjoying out of obligation, the phone will win every night and you will conclude you cannot read any more. Abandon it. The habit matters more than the title.

Where in the day to put it

Most people try to read at the worst available moment, which is in bed after a full day, competing directly against a phone at the hour when reading is hardest and scrolling is easiest.

Two better slots. The first is the twenty minutes before you go to bed rather than once you are in it, sitting somewhere that is not the bed, with the phone already in another room. Reading in a chair works far better than reading lying down, because lying down is where your capacity for concentration has already gone.

The second is the morning. Ten minutes with coffee before anything from the internet reaches you. You are rested, nothing is competing, and the starting cost of two pages is much lower than it will be at eleven at night. This is also why a phone free morning tends to produce reading without anybody having resolved to read.

The advice that reliably fails

Reading targets. Fifty books a year turns reading into admin, and the counting starts to matter more than the reading. People hit the target with short books they did not like.

Reading on your phone. It sounds efficient and it is the strongest version of the trap, because the thing you are escaping is one swipe away at every moment of difficulty. A dedicated e reader is fine. The phone is not.

Waiting for more time. You have the time. It is the hour you are currently scrolling. Nothing new needs to be found.

Streaks. Worth knowing that the research does not support the logic. Lally and colleagues found that missing a single day did not appear to reduce habit formation,1 which contradicts the belief that one lapse undoes your progress. That belief is the most common reason people quit.

What the evidence says about the swap

Nobody has run a trial on reading versus scrolling specifically, and anyone quoting one is inventing it.

What has been measured is adjacent. A randomised study of 2,844 Facebook users found that four weeks of verified deactivation increased offline activity, including socialising with family and friends and also watching television alone.2

Note the television. Freed time does not automatically become reading. It goes wherever unstructured time goes, which is why the book needs to be physically there rather than merely intended.

And on durability: in a 2025 randomised trial, students who held phone use under two hours a day for three weeks improved on stress, wellbeing, depression and sleep, then returned towards baseline once the limit ended.3 Make the arrangement permanent and small.

A last note on format. If paper is losing to the phone, a dedicated e reader is a legitimate middle option, because it has the availability of a device and none of the escape routes. The screen is dull, there is nothing else on it, and crucially nobody can message you on it.

Tonight: phone charging in the kitchen, a book on the bedside table, and a block on your apps from half an hour before bed. Read one page.

Read more instead of scrolling: FAQs

How do I read more instead of scrolling?

Change availability rather than relying on preference. Put the phone in another room, put a book where the phone was, and block your apps automatically from half an hour before bed.

Why do I always choose my phone over a book?

Three asymmetries: the phone is closer, reading has a starting cost that scrolling does not, and you can scroll when too tired to read but not the reverse.

Should I read on my phone to make it easier?

No. It is the strongest version of the trap, since the thing you are escaping is one swipe away at every difficult moment. A dedicated e reader is fine.

How much should I aim to read?

One page. It is small enough that you cannot reasonably refuse and it gets you past the starting cost, which is where almost all the resistance actually lives.

Do reading streaks help?

The research suggests they are not necessary. Lally and colleagues found that missing a single day did not appear to reduce habit formation, so a lapse is not a reset.

What if I am not enjoying the book?

Abandon it. Persisting out of obligation guarantees the phone wins every night, and the habit matters considerably more than finishing any particular title.
