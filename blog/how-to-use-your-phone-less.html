How to use your phone less

Nine changes, ordered by how much they actually do. The first three are worth more than the other six combined.

By Jacob Redshaw · 14 September 2026 · 7 min read

Quick answer

To use your phone less, change the situation rather than your intentions. Block specific hours automatically, charge the phone in another room, delete one app for two weeks, and turn off every notification that is not from a person. Decide once, in advance, rather than repeatedly.

Almost all advice on how to use your phone less fails for the same reason. It asks you to behave differently thousands of times a day, using a faculty that runs out by about 4pm.

What follows is ordered by how much each thing actually does. The first three are worth more than the rest put together, and the last two are near the line of being worth bothering with at all.

Why intentions do not survive

Phone use is not one decision. It is roughly one decision per second for as long as the phone is in your hand, and you have to win every single one. A 99 percent success rate over an hour still ends with you scrolling.

So anything shaped like "I will be more mindful about my phone" is asking to win an unlimited run of coin flips. You will not. Nobody does.

The things that work all share one property: they make the decision once, in advance, when you are capable, and then remove you from the loop.

Nine ways to use your phone less, in order

1. Block specific hours, automatically

The single highest value change. Pick the hours you keep losing, usually the one before bed and the first one of the morning, and have your chosen apps become unavailable then without you doing anything. Apple's Screen Time Schedule does this for free and is already on your phone.

Hours beat days. An hour you reclaim repeats 365 times a year. A heroic weekend repeats once.

2. Charge the phone in another room

Costs about ten pounds for an alarm clock and beats most software. Physical distance is the most reliable friction there is, because it cannot be dismissed with a tap. This matters most at night: the American Academy of Sleep Medicine surveyed 2,007 US adults in 2025 and found most were choosing screen time over sleep at bedtime.1

3. Delete one app for two weeks

Not forever, which feels too big to agree to. Two weeks. Pick the worst one.

Two weeks is long enough to break the reflex and short enough that you will actually try it. Most people do not reinstall, and that is information you cannot get any other way.

4. Turn off every notification that is not from a person

Keep calls and messages from humans. Kill everything sent by a company to bring you back. Most phone sessions are not chosen, they are triggered, and this removes most of the triggers.

5. Remove the apps from your home screen

Do not delete, just move them off the first screen so you have to search for them. Typing the name converts a reflex into a decision, and a surprising number of reaches die in that gap.

6. Log out after every use

Annoying, which is the point. Having to enter a password is a small deliberate act, and it is a lot of the reason desktop social media use is less compulsive than mobile.

7. Give the phone a place to live

A bowl by the door, a drawer in the kitchen. Not on you, not face up on the table. If it has a home, picking it up becomes a visible act rather than a default state.

8. Replace rather than only remove

You reach for the phone in specific gaps: queues, adverts, the ten minutes before sleep. If nothing fills those gaps you will go back. Have a book within reach of your bed. It does not need to be a good book.

9. Grayscale

Last on the list deliberately. It makes the phone duller and some people find it genuinely helps, but the evidence is thin and the effect is probably small. Try it, do not rely on it, and do not let it be the only thing you change.

What the research supports

Using your phone less has measured benefits. A 2025 randomised controlled trial in BMC Medicine took 111 students averaging 276 minutes a day, and had 58 of them hold under two hours a day for three weeks. Against the control group they improved on stress, wellbeing, depressive symptoms and sleep quality, with small to medium effect sizes.2

How to pick a target that holds

  1. Look at your real weekly average in Settings, then Screen Time. Use the number on the screen, not the one you would say out loud.
  2. Take 20 percent off it. On five hours that is four. It will feel unambitious, which is why it works.
  3. Hold it a fortnight, then take another 20 percent off.
  4. Stop when it starts to pinch.

Five hours to four is 365 hours a year. Nobody is ever impressed by that target, and it beats the one hour goal you abandon on Thursday.

If you only do one thing: block the hour before bed, automatically, tonight. It is free, it repeats every night, and it is the hour with the least to defend it.

Use your phone less: FAQs

How can I use my phone less without deleting everything?

Block specific hours automatically rather than cutting apps entirely. The hour before bed and the first hour of the morning are the cheapest to give up and they repeat every day, which is where the value is.

How long does it take to break a phone habit?

There is no reliable number, and anyone quoting one precisely is guessing. What the evidence does show is that usage returns to baseline once a limit ends, so plan for something permanent rather than a fixed detox period.

Does grayscale actually reduce screen time?

Possibly a little. Some people find it genuinely helps, but the evidence is thin and the effect is likely small. Treat it as a minor addition rather than your main change.

What is a realistic screen time target?

Take 20 percent off your current weekly average rather than choosing a round number. Cutting five hours to four is 365 hours a year and you will actually keep it.

Should I do a digital detox?

A detox gives you a good week and then ends, and in the one trial that followed up, usage returned towards baseline. A smaller permanent change beats a short strict one.

Why do notifications matter so much?

Because most phone sessions are triggered rather than chosen. Turning off everything sent by a company, while keeping messages and calls from people, removes most of the triggers without cutting you off.
