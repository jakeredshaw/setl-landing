Time Allowances vs Screen Time Schedule: which to use

Two settings, next to each other, doing opposite jobs. Picking the wrong one is the commonest reason people think Apple's blocking is useless.

By Jacob Redshaw · 14 September 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer

On Time Allowances vs Screen Time Schedule: an allowance is a daily budget that reports when your time is spent and lets you continue, while a schedule makes apps unavailable during hours you set in advance. For a specific problem hour, choose the schedule.

These two settings sit next to each other in iOS 27 and do fundamentally different jobs. The question of time allowances vs screen time schedule is worth two minutes of thought, because choosing wrong is the most common reason people decide Apple's tools do not work.

Apple's guide points you to both from the same screen: tap Screen Time, then tap Screen Time Schedule or Time Allowances.1 Nothing tells you which one your problem calls for.

One reports. The other prevents. That is the whole distinction and almost everything follows from it.

What Time Allowances is good at

Awareness, and a soft ceiling on the total.

In iOS 27 it works as a single daily budget spanning all categories, which you can then fine tune per category.2 That is a genuine improvement on the old per app limits, which had a hole in them: you could be inside every individual limit and still have spent five hours, because the limits did not add up.

Choose an allowance if your honest problem is that you did not know where the time went. A surprising amount of phone use is unexamined rather than compulsive, and for those people simply seeing the number changes the behaviour.

What Screen Time Schedule is good at

Specific hours you keep losing.

A schedule decides in advance, which means it is enforced by the version of you who set it up rather than the version of you at midnight. That asymmetry is the entire value. An allowance has to be respected by the person who is tired and wants to carry on. A schedule has already acted before that person shows up.

iOS 27 schedules also support separate weekday and weekend routines, and let you choose which apps are accessible in a given window.2 The weekend routine is the most underused setting in the whole feature, because a single bedtime applied to all seven nights is wrong on Friday, so people disable it on Friday and never turn it back on.

Why most people pick the allowance and then give up

Because it is the intuitive choice. The problem presents itself as too much time, so a time limit sounds like the answer.

Then this happens. You are on five hours a day, you set a one hour allowance, you blow through it before lunch on day one, you tap past it, and by Thursday you have stopped noticing the notification. Now you have trained yourself to dismiss it, which is worse than where you started, because dismissal generalises.

If you do use an allowance, set it just under your current average rather than at your aspiration. Take 20 percent off the number in Settings, hold that for a fortnight, then take another 20 percent off.

There is a second, subtler reason the allowance disappoints people. It arrives at the worst possible moment to be persuasive. By the time your budget is spent you are already inside the behaviour, mid scroll, with the thing in your hand, and you are being asked to stop by a notification. Nothing about that moment favours the notification. A schedule never has to win that argument, because it acts while you are still in the kitchen deciding whether to go upstairs.

None of which makes allowances useless. It makes them a measurement tool that happens to have a weak enforcement feature attached. Used as measurement, they are genuinely good, and the single daily budget in iOS 27 is a real improvement. Used as enforcement, they will keep letting you down and you will keep concluding something is broken.

Use both, in the right roles

They are not alternatives. The sensible setup is a schedule for the hours that matter and an allowance as a loose ceiling on everything else.

  1. Set a Screen Time Schedule covering bedtime to an hour after you wake.
  2. Add a separate weekend routine, generous enough that you never switch it off.
  3. Set a daily allowance at 20 percent below your current average.
  4. Empty your Always Allowed list down to Phone and Messages, or neither setting will do anything.
  5. Use a passcode you have not memorised.
Shortest possible advice: if you already know where your time goes, you need a schedule. If you genuinely do not, start with an allowance.

Time allowances vs screen time schedule: FAQs

What is the difference between Time Allowances and Screen Time Schedule?

An allowance is a daily budget that reports when your time is spent and then lets you continue. A schedule makes apps unavailable during hours you set in advance. One reports, the other prevents.

Which one should I use?

If you already know exactly where your time goes, use a schedule. If you genuinely do not know, start with an allowance, because awareness may be enough on its own.

Can I use both at once?

Yes, and that is the best setup. Use a schedule for the hours that matter, such as bedtime to an hour after waking, and an allowance as a loose ceiling on the rest of the day.

Why does my Time Allowance not stop me?

Because it is not designed to. When the budget runs out it informs you and permits you to carry on. If you want apps to become genuinely unavailable, you need a schedule.

What were these called before?

Time Allowances was App Limits and Screen Time Schedule was Downtime. Apple renamed both in iOS 27, which is why older guides describe menus that no longer match your phone.

What allowance should I set?

Twenty percent below your current weekly average, not your ideal. A budget you breach daily becomes a notification you learn to dismiss, and that habit of dismissing generalises.
