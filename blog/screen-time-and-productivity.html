Screen time and productivity

The productivity claims in this industry are almost all invented. Here is what has actually been measured, and what follows from it.

By Jacob Redshaw · 14 September 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer

On screen time and productivity, no randomised trial has shown that cutting phone use increases work output. What has been measured is that reducing phone use improves stress, mood and sleep modestly, and those may help concentration indirectly. That is a weaker claim than the usual one.

Almost everything written about screen time and productivity contains a number that nobody can source. Percentages of lost output, minutes of recovery, hours reclaimed. Most of it is invented, repeated, and then cited as if the repetition were evidence.

So here is the honest version, including the part that does not help me sell anything.

What has not been shown

No randomised trial has demonstrated that blocking phone apps increases work output.

That is a simple statement and it rules out most of the marketing in this category, including claims I could easily make about my own product. If somebody tells you a blocker will make you 30 percent more productive, ask them for the study. There is not one.

Productivity is also genuinely hard to measure outside a factory. For most knowledge work there is no agreed unit of output, which is part of why the research does not exist rather than being negative.

That absence gets filled in a predictable way. Vendors cite studies about interruption, or about multitasking, or about sleep, and let the reader assemble a conclusion the studies never stated. Technically nothing false has been said. The effect is a whole category of claims that sound evidence based and are not, and the people most harmed by it are the ones who buy a tool, work exactly as badly as before, and conclude the failure is theirs.

What has been measured

The wellbeing side, and reasonably well.

A 2025 randomised controlled trial in BMC Medicine took 111 healthy students with a baseline of 276 minutes of phone use a day. 58 of them held under two hours a day for three weeks. Against the control group they improved on stress, wellbeing, depressive symptoms and sleep quality, with small to medium effect sizes.1

Lower stress and better sleep plausibly help you think. But that is an indirect argument and it should be labelled as one. Nobody measured whether those students got more done.

There is also a measured effect on how people spend their time. A randomised study of 2,844 Facebook users found that four weeks of verified deactivation increased offline activity, including socialising with family and friends and watching television alone.2

Note the television. The time freed up did not automatically go into anything productive. That finding deserves far more attention than it gets, because the implicit promise of every focus tool is that reclaimed hours become valuable hours, and in the one study that looked, some of them became television.

This matters for how you set expectations with yourself. People buy a blocker imagining the version of their week where the two reclaimed hours go into the side project, and then feel they have failed when the hours go into cooking properly and watching something. Nothing has failed. The time came back and it went where unstructured time goes. If you want it somewhere specific, that is a separate piece of work from blocking anything, and it is the harder half.

The 23 minute claim

You will see it everywhere: 23 minutes and 15 seconds to refocus after an interruption.

Your phone can go to sleep first.

SETL blocks the apps you choose, at bedtime and any hour you set.

Treat it sceptically. The figure traces back to a small observational study of office workers, not a controlled experiment on phone use, and the precision is doing work it has not earned. Task switching does cost something real, but you do not need a fabricated number to know that forty interruptions is worse than six.

More on this in the 23 minute claim examined. Quoting a suspiciously precise figure from a study you have not read is how a field loses the right to be cited.

What screen time and productivity advice should actually say

Three things, at the confidence the evidence supports.

Do it for the sleep and the stress, not for the output. Those have been measured. Output has not. If better sleep then helps your work, good, but lead with what is true.

Decide what the reclaimed time is for, in advance. This follows directly from the television finding. Freed hours do not have a purpose by default, and the phone is not the only passive option available.

Judge the arrangement in March. In the BMC Medicine trial, screen time returned towards baseline once the limit ended.1 A productive fortnight is not the thing you want.

What phone reaching usually tells you

One practical observation that costs nothing to test. The reach rarely arrives when your phone buzzes. It arrives at the exact moment the work gets difficult: the paragraph that will not come out, the decision you have to make, the thing you do not want to find out.

If that is the pattern, the phone is a symptom. Unclear next steps, unclear ownership and quiet dread all produce identical reaching behaviour, and no blocker addresses any of them. People with elaborate focus setups and no output are usually solving the wrong problem carefully.

The useful question when you notice heavy reaching is not how do I resist this. It is what about this task am I avoiding.

The honest pitch: blocking your phone will probably help you sleep and feel slightly better, and may help you concentrate as a consequence. Anyone offering you a productivity percentage is making it up.

Screen time and productivity: FAQs

Does reducing screen time make you more productive?

No trial has shown it. What has been measured is that cutting phone use improves stress, mood and sleep modestly, which may help concentration indirectly. That is a weaker claim than the usual one.

Do app blockers improve work output?

There is no evidence that they do. Productivity is also hard to measure in knowledge work, which is part of why the research does not exist rather than having come back negative.

Is the 23 minute refocus statistic real?

Treat it carefully. It traces to a small observational study of office workers rather than a controlled experiment on phones, and the precision implies more rigour than the source supports.

What happens to the time you free up?

Not necessarily anything useful. A randomised deactivation study found freed time went into socialising with family and friends, and also into watching television alone.

Why do I reach for my phone when working?

Usually at the moment the work becomes difficult rather than when a notification arrives. The phone is the nearest exit from discomfort, which makes it a symptom as much as a cause.

So is blocking apps worth doing?

For sleep and stress, the evidence says probably yes, modestly. For output, nobody knows. Decide in advance what the reclaimed time is for, or it will not go anywhere in particular.
