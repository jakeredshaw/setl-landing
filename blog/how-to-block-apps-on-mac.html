Screen Time

How to block apps on Mac

If you work at a desk all day, blocking your phone is half the job at best. Here is the other half.

By Jacob Redshaw · 14 September 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer

To block apps on Mac, open System Settings and go to Screen Time, then set a schedule for when apps should be unavailable. On a computer the website restrictions matter more than the app ones, because almost everything distracting runs in a browser.

You block apps on mac through Screen Time in System Settings, and it works much as it does on an iPhone. Apple says the latest Screen Time features require macOS 27, iOS 27, iPadOS 27 or later.1

The important difference is not the settings. It is that on a computer, app blocking is the less useful half.

Why websites matter more than apps on a Mac

On a phone, the app is the main route to a service and the website is the loophole. On a Mac it is the reverse. Almost everything that costs you an afternoon runs in a browser tab, and blocking a desktop app achieves very little when the same service is one tab away.

So do the website restrictions first and treat the app blocking as secondary. This is the opposite order from the phone and it is the mistake most people make when they extend their setup to a computer.

How to block apps on Mac, step by step

  1. Open System Settings.
  2. Go to Screen Time. Turn it on if it is not already.
  3. Set a schedule for the hours you want apps and sites unavailable.
  4. Use the content restrictions to limit web content, and add the specific sites you lose time to.
  5. Set a Screen Time passcode, and use one you have not memorised.
  6. Check what is in your allowed list, since anything exempt will ignore the schedule entirely, exactly as on iPhone.

If you use a browser other than Safari, test whether the restrictions hold in it. They are strongest in Safari and handled inconsistently elsewhere, which on a Mac usually means Chrome is a complete bypass. The honest fix is often to uninstall the second browser rather than to configure around it.

On a computer this is unusually effective, because typing a password is a deliberate act and most distraction is reflexive. A tab you are already logged into is one click. A tab that wants credentials is a decision, and a decision is where the reflex dies.

What to actually block on a computer

The list differs from a phone, because the things eating a working day on a Mac mostly look like work.

Email. Other people's priorities arriving in real time. Answering it feels productive while reliably preventing the harder work. Block it during focused stretches rather than all day.

Team chat. The hardest one socially, because being unavailable has a visible cost. Block it for ninety minutes, tell people that is what you do, and the expectation adjusts faster than you expect.

News and social in the browser. The obvious ones, and on a Mac they are browser tabs rather than apps, which is why the website restrictions carry the weight.

Anything with a badge count. Badges are a standing invitation to check, and on a large screen several of them are visible at once.

Three Mac specific traps

Work accounts. If your machine is managed by an employer, some Screen Time settings may be controlled centrally or unavailable. Check before assuming a setting failed.

Two browsers. Covered above and worth repeating, because it is the single most common reason a Mac setup does nothing.

The phone sitting next to the Mac. Blocking your computer while an unblocked phone is beside the keyboard moves the behaviour by eighteen inches. Both devices or neither.

Session length on a computer

Blocking works better in defined stretches than all day, and the reason is the same as on a phone. An indefinite block feels like deprivation, gets resented, and is dismantled the first time you legitimately need something inside it. Once off, it stays off.

Ninety minutes suits writing, coding or analysis. Twenty five suits admin and email, where the dread rather than the focus is the problem. Put two of the long ones in your calendar as repeating events so starting one is not a decision made while already avoiding work.

What the evidence supports, and what it does not

No trial has shown that blocking apps on a computer increases work output. I am not going to imply otherwise.

What has been measured is phone use and wellbeing. A 2025 randomised controlled trial in BMC Medicine had 58 students hold phone use under two hours a day for three weeks, with improvements in stress, wellbeing, depressive symptoms and sleep quality against a control group, and usage returning towards baseline once the limit ended.2

That is about phones rather than computers, so take it as adjacent rather than as evidence for this page. The one finding that transfers cleanly is the durability point: whatever you set up has to survive your motivation running out.

Block apps on mac: FAQs

How do I block apps on my Mac?

Open System Settings, go to Screen Time, turn it on and set a schedule for when apps should be unavailable. Then use content restrictions to block specific websites.

Should I block apps or websites on a Mac?

Websites first. On a computer almost everything distracting runs in a browser tab, so blocking a desktop app achieves little while the same service is one tab away.

Do Mac website blocks work in Chrome?

Often not. Restrictions are strongest in Safari and inconsistent elsewhere, so on a Mac a second browser is usually a complete bypass. Uninstalling it is the honest fix.

What works better than Screen Time on a Mac?

Logging out of everything at the end of each day. Typing a password is deliberate and most distraction is reflexive, so credentials are where the reflex dies.

Why do my Mac Screen Time settings not apply?

If the machine is managed by an employer, some settings may be controlled centrally or unavailable. Check that before concluding the feature is broken.

Does SETL work on Mac?

No. SETL is iPhone only, so use Apple's Screen Time for a Mac. Blocking a computer while an unblocked phone sits beside it moves the behaviour rather than reducing it.
