Phone addiction: the signs, and how to break the habit

You pick up your phone without deciding to, and put it down an hour later. Is that addiction? Here is what the research actually says.

By Jacob Redshaw · 14 September 2026 · 5 min read

Quick answer

Phone addiction is not a formal diagnosis in the DSM-5-TR, and many researchers prefer the term problematic smartphone use. Commonly studied signs include using your phone longer than intended, feeling uneasy without it, and carrying on despite harm to daily life. Turning off notifications, hiding apps and scheduled blocks can help you cut back.

Is phone addiction a real diagnosis?

Phone addiction is a common way of describing smartphone use that feels out of control. But it is not a formal diagnosis. The American Psychiatric Association says gambling disorder is the only behavioural addiction identified in the DSM-5-TR, the manual clinicians use to diagnose mental disorders.1

Internet gaming disorder is listed in a section for conditions that need further research. The APA notes that this proposed condition covers gaming only, and does not include general internet use or use of social media or smartphones.1 Montag and colleagues also point out that "smartphone addiction" is not recognised in the WHO's ICD-11 either.2

Smartphone addiction or problematic smartphone use?

Because the addiction label is contested, many researchers use the term problematic smartphone use instead.2 A 2018 review by Panova and Carbonell did not find enough support to confirm smartphone addiction exists. They argued the behaviour is better called problematic or maladaptive use, and that its consequences do not reach the severity of addiction.3

That does not mean the problem is imaginary. It means the words matter. You can have a real problem with your phone without having a medical addiction.

Phone addiction signs researchers look for

One well known research tool is the Smartphone Addiction Scale Short Version (SAS-SV). A South Korean research team built it from a longer scale and tested it with 540 adolescents.4 It has 10 statements, each rated from strongly disagree to strongly agree.5 In plain English, they cover:

  1. Missing planned work because of your phone
  2. Struggling to concentrate in class, on assignments or at work because of it
  3. Pain in your wrists or the back of your neck while using it
  4. Feeling you could not stand being without it
  5. Feeling impatient and fretful when you are not holding it
  6. Having it on your mind even when you are not using it
  7. Refusing to give it up even when it is badly affecting daily life
  8. Constantly checking so you do not miss conversations on social media
  9. Using it for longer than you intended
  10. People around you telling you that you use it too much

The item wording above is paraphrased from the published list.5 The authors were clear that the scale identifies risk level. It is not designed to diagnose smartphone addiction.4

Am I addicted to my phone?

Read the list honestly. If several statements sound like you, especially the ones about losing control and carrying on despite harm, your phone use is probably working against you.

A good question to ask is not "how many hours?" but "is my phone getting in the way of things I care about?" Sleep, work, study and time with people are the usual places it shows. Our guide on how much screen time is too much looks at the numbers side.

What is problematic smartphone use linked to?

A 2019 systematic review in BMC Psychiatry included 41 studies covering 41,871 children and young people. The median prevalence of problematic smartphone use was 23.3%, roughly one in four.6

It was associated with higher odds of depression, anxiety, higher perceived stress and poorer sleep quality.6 Most of the included studies were cross sectional, so these are associations. They do not show that phones cause these problems, and the link may run both ways.

Why phones are so hard to put down

Montag and colleagues argue that the smartphone itself is not the main culprit. It is the apps, many of which are built to hold attention because more time in the app means more data and better targeted ads.2 Endless feeds, likes and read receipts all pull you back. We cover the details in why apps are addictive.

How to break phone addiction: practical steps

In a 2022 trial at McGill University, mostly with students, people chose from ten "nudge" strategies. Compared with a group who only tracked their screen time, they reduced problematic smartphone use and screen time and improved sleep quality.7 Several of these steps come from that study.

  1. Turn off non essential notifications. This was the strategy people in the trial were most likely to stick with.7
  2. Put it out of sight. Keep your phone silent, face down and out of reach when you are not using it.7
  3. Hide the pull apps. Move social media and email apps into a folder off your home screen.7
  4. Add a little friction. The trial suggested using a passcode instead of Face ID, and switching to greyscale.7
  5. Keep it out of reach at bedtime. Silent and out of reach when you go to bed.7
  6. Aim for less, not zero. In a German experiment with 619 smartphone users, cutting use by one hour a day reduced depressive and anxiety symptoms, with mostly stronger and more stable effects than a week of total abstinence.8
  7. Schedule blocks. A screen time blocker can lock your most tempting apps at set times, so you are not relying on willpower alone. See also how to reduce screen time on iPhone.

If your phone use sits alongside low mood, anxiety or poor sleep, speak to your GP. Cutting back can help, but it is not a replacement for support.

Phone addiction: FAQs

Is phone addiction a real thing?

Phone addiction is not a formal diagnosis in the DSM-5-TR or ICD-11. Researchers do study problematic smartphone use, which is linked to anxiety, depression, stress and poorer sleep.

What are the signs of smartphone addiction?

Common signs on research scales include using your phone longer than intended, feeling impatient without it, thinking about it when not using it, missing planned work and people telling you that you use it too much.

Am I addicted to my phone?

Only a clinician can assess you, and phone addiction is not a diagnosis. If your phone regularly gets in the way of sleep, work, study or relationships, it is worth cutting back.

How common is problematic smartphone use?

A 2019 review of 41 studies of children and young people found a median prevalence of 23.3%, roughly one in four.

How do I break phone addiction?

Turn off non essential notifications, keep your phone out of sight, hide social apps off your home screen and aim to cut an hour a day rather than quitting. Scheduled app blocks can take willpower out of it.

Should I quit my smartphone completely?

You may not need to. In one German experiment, cutting use by an hour a day gave mostly stronger and more stable benefits over four months than a week without a smartphone.
