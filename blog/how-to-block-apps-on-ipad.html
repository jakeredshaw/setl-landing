How to block apps on iPad

The settings are the same as iPhone with different names, and one extra trap that catches almost everybody with more than one device.

By Jacob Redshaw · 14 September 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer

To block apps on iPad, open Settings, tap Screen Time, then tap Screen Time Schedule to make your chosen apps unavailable during set hours, or Time Allowances to set a daily budget instead. These two replaced Downtime and App Limits in iPadOS 27, and the menu names changed with them.

You block apps on ipad the same way you do on iPhone, through Screen Time, with one complication that catches most people who own both devices.

Apple renamed the settings in iPadOS 27. Downtime is now Screen Time Schedule and App Limits are now Time Allowances, and Apple says all the latest Screen Time features need iPadOS 27, iOS 27, macOS 27 or later.1

How to block apps on iPad for set hours

  1. Open Settings.
  2. Tap Screen Time. If you have never used it, turn it on and let it gather a few days of data first.
  3. Tap Screen Time Schedule.
  4. Turn it on and set the hours you want your chosen apps unavailable.
  5. Set a separate weekend routine if yours differs.
  6. Go back and open Always Allowed. Strip it down to essentials. Anything on that list ignores your schedule entirely.
  7. Set a Screen Time passcode that you have not memorised.

How to set a daily budget instead

If your problem is total time rather than specific hours, use Time Allowances. In iPadOS 27 it works as one daily budget spanning all categories, which you can then fine tune per category.2

Be aware of what an allowance does when you reach it. It tells you the time is spent and then lets you continue. If you want apps to actually become unavailable, use the schedule instead. The difference is covered in Time Allowances versus Screen Time Schedule.

On a tablet the single daily budget is more useful than it is on a phone, because tablet use tends to come in long single app stretches rather than hundreds of short pickups. One number across everything catches a three hour evening in a way that per app limits never did, since each individual app stays comfortably inside its own allowance while the evening disappears.

This is the iPad specific part and it goes both ways.

If you have Screen Time sharing turned on across devices, your settings and your reported usage can be combined. That produces two confusing outcomes.

Your reported total looks wrong. The headline figure may be adding your iPad and iPhone together, which is why people think their phone use has exploded when they bought a tablet.

A block you set on one device may not behave as expected on the other. Check both devices after setting anything up, rather than assuming. Open the schedule on the iPad itself and confirm the specific apps you care about are in it.

Apple also notes that settings sync across a child's devices in a Family Sharing setup,2 which is useful for parents and surprising if you were configuring your own tablet and did not expect changes to propagate.

Why it is harder to block apps on iPad than on iPhone

Three reasons worth knowing before you start.

The iPad is where you do the thing you blocked. People block social apps on their phone and then scroll the same services on the tablet in the evening. If you only block one device, you have moved the behaviour rather than reduced it.

The browser matters more. On a larger screen the website version of an app is genuinely pleasant to use, so blocking the app alone achieves very little. You will need Web Content restrictions too, covered in how to block websites, which works the same way on iPad.

It is often a shared device. If the iPad is used by more than one person, a passcode you have given to someone else or a schedule set for the household gets dismantled by whoever needs it dismantled first.

That third point is worth planning around rather than fighting. On a genuinely shared tablet, a restriction aimed at your own habits will keep being removed for entirely legitimate reasons, and each removal costs you the arrangement. If that is your situation, a per user approach works better: use your own Apple account on the device if possible, or accept that the tablet is not where this problem gets solved and concentrate on the device that is only yours.

There is also a quieter reason iPads defeat people. Tablet use does not feel like phone use. It feels like reading, or watching something, or sitting down properly with a screen, and that framing makes the hours much easier to justify. The report does not care how it felt. If you are blocking your phone and the total across both devices has not moved, nothing has actually changed.

Block apps on ipad: FAQs

How do I block apps on my iPad?

Settings, then Screen Time, then Screen Time Schedule to make apps unavailable during set hours. Time Allowances gives you a daily budget instead. Both replaced Downtime and App Limits in iPadOS 27.

Why can I not find Downtime on my iPad?

It was renamed. Downtime is now Screen Time Schedule and App Limits are now Time Allowances. Apple says the latest Screen Time features need iPadOS 27 or later.

Does blocking apps on my iPhone also block them on my iPad?

Not reliably, and this is the most common mistake. Check each device separately and confirm the specific apps are in the schedule on the iPad itself.

Why does my iPad screen time look so high?

If Screen Time is shared across devices, the headline figure may be combining your iPad and iPhone. Look at the per device and per app breakdown rather than the total.

Do I need to block websites on iPad too?

Yes, more so than on a phone. On a larger screen the website version of a blocked app is genuinely usable, so app blocking alone achieves very little without Web Content restrictions.

Is there a third party app blocker for iPad?

Some exist, but SETL is iPhone only, so Apple's built in Screen Time is the route described here. Check any third party app's listing for iPad support before buying.
