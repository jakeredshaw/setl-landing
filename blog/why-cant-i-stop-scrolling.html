Why can't I stop scrolling?

It is not weakness and it is not a lack of discipline. The feed is built so that no moment ever arrives where stopping is the obvious thing to do.

By Jacob Redshaw · 14 September 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer

You cannot stop scrolling because an endless feed never presents a stopping point. Every other medium has an end that prompts a decision. A feed removes that prompt, so continuing is the default and stopping requires an act of will you have to repeat indefinitely.

The reason you cannot stop scrolling is not that you are weak. It is that you are being asked to make a decision that nothing in the design ever prompts you to make.

Compare it to anything else you consume. A book has chapters. An episode ends and the credits roll. A newspaper runs out of pages. Each of those gives you a natural moment where carrying on becomes an active choice, and in that gap you usually choose correctly.

A feed has no such moment. There is no end, so there is no gap, so no decision is ever triggered. You do not choose to continue. You simply never arrive at the place where you would have chosen to stop.

Why willpower is the wrong tool for this

Willpower works on discrete decisions. Should I have the second slice. Should I go to the gym now. One choice, one answer, done.

Scrolling is not one decision. It is roughly one decision per second, for as long as you hold the phone, and you have to win every single one. A 99 percent success rate over forty minutes still ends with you scrolling, because one failure is all it takes and then the counter resets.

Framed that way it is obvious why you lose. You are not failing at a hard task. You are being asked to win an unlimited number of coin flips in a row.

There is a second problem on top of that one. Each individual swipe is genuinely trivial. Nobody has ever ruined their life with one more post, and that is true every single time you tell it to yourself, which is what makes it such an effective argument. The cost only exists in aggregate, and aggregates are invisible in the moment. You cannot feel the fortieth minute arriving. You can only see it afterwards, in a weekly report you will glance at and close.

That is also why resolving to do better rarely changes anything. The resolution is made once, in a calm moment. The test happens thousands of times, in moments that are not calm.

Four reasons it is worse at night

  1. You are depleted. Whatever capacity you had for resisting things has been spent on the day.
  2. Nothing is scheduled next. At 2pm, something comes after this. At 11pm, nothing does, so there is no external force ending the session.
  3. The alternative is unpleasant. The alternative to scrolling in bed is lying in the dark with your own thoughts, which the scrolling is partly there to avoid.
  4. Nobody is watching. Including you, in any meaningful sense. There is no record and no consequence until morning.

Survey data suggests this is close to universal rather than unusual. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine surveyed 2,007 US adults in 2025 and found most were choosing screen time over sleep at bedtime.1 If it feels like a personal failing, it is worth knowing how many people share the identical failing.

What the research says about doing something about it

Cutting back genuinely helps. A 2025 randomised controlled trial in BMC Medicine took 111 students averaging 276 minutes of phone use a day, and asked 58 of them to hold under two hours a day for three weeks. Compared with the control group they improved on stress, wellbeing, depressive symptoms and sleep quality.2

How to stop scrolling, in order of what works

Create the stopping point the feed refuses to give you

This is the whole game. If no natural end exists, impose one from outside, at a fixed time, decided in advance. Not a limit you can wave away. An actual end.

Move the decision to the afternoon

Decide at 2pm what happens at 11pm. The 2pm version of you is competent and has opinions about your life. The 11pm version has neither. Any system that relies on the 11pm version is badly designed.

Make the phone physically distant

Charger in the kitchen, alarm clock in the bedroom. Free, boring, and it does genuine work because it adds a walk between you and the behaviour.

Remove the app rather than resolving to use it less

Deleting one app for a fortnight tells you more than a month of intending to cut down. Most people find they do not reinstall it.

Grayscale, maybe

It makes the phone duller and some people swear by it. The evidence is thin and the effect is probably small. Try it, but do not expect it to carry the whole thing.

The one sentence version: stop trying to win the decision and change the situation so the decision never comes up.

Stop scrolling: FAQs

Why can't I stop scrolling even when I want to?

Because an endless feed never gives you a stopping point. Every other medium ends and prompts a decision. A feed removes the prompt, so continuing is the default and you have to actively interrupt yourself instead.

Is being unable to stop scrolling a sign of addiction?

Not necessarily. Compulsive feed use is extremely common and is better explained by how feeds are designed than by anything unusual about you. Speak to a doctor if it is affecting your work, sleep or relationships.

Why is scrolling worse at night?

You are depleted, nothing is scheduled after it, the alternative is lying in the dark with your thoughts, and there is no immediate consequence. All four push the same direction at once.

Does deleting social media apps work?

Often, yes, and better than intending to use them less. Removing one app for two weeks gives you real information, and most people find they do not reinstall it.

Will more willpower fix scrolling?

Unlikely, because scrolling is not one decision. It is a decision every second that you have to win every time. One loss is enough, so even a very high success rate still ends with you scrolling.

What is the single most effective change?

Creating an external stopping point at a fixed time, decided in advance. Anything that depends on you choosing well at midnight is relying on the least capable version of you.
