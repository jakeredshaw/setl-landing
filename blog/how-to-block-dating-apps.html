How to block dating apps on iPhone

Blocking the hours works. Before you do it, it is worth being clear about which of two problems you actually have.

By Jacob Redshaw · 14 September 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer

To block dating apps on iPhone, set a Screen Time Schedule covering the evening and night, and add the matching websites to Never Allow. Deleting the apps is better supported by evidence if you are using them compulsively rather than deliberately.

You can block dating apps on iPhone in a few minutes. The more useful question is which problem you are solving, because there are two and they need different answers.

The two problems

One: you are using them deliberately and it is going badly. You open them, you swipe with intent, the experience is demoralising. Here the apps are working as designed and the issue is the design. Blocking hours will not help much. Taking a proper break probably will.

Two: you are using them compulsively. You open them in gaps, without deciding to, forty times a day, mostly not reading anything. Here it is a feed habit that happens to be attached to dating, and it responds to the same treatment as any other feed.

Check your pickup count in Settings, then Screen Time. If the dating apps are among the first things you open each time you lift the phone, you have the second problem.

How to block dating apps for set hours

  1. Settings, then Screen Time, then Screen Time Schedule.1
  2. Turn it on, covering the evening and night. Late evening is where almost all the compulsive use sits.
  3. Select the apps, and check whether they sit in the Social category, since app classification does not always match intuition.
  4. Add the websites under Content and Privacy Restrictions, then Web Content, then Never Allow.2
  5. Strip your Always Allowed list and remove any browser from it.
  6. Turn off every notification from them. A match alert is a trigger you did not choose, and it arrives designed to be opened immediately.

Why the late night use is the expensive part

Three conditions converge after about ten at night, and they are the same ones that make everything else on your phone harder at that hour.

Your judgement is depleted. Nothing is scheduled afterwards to end the session. And the alternative is lying in the dark with your own thoughts, which, if you are single and would rather not be, is precisely the thing the app is being used to avoid.

That last point is the one worth sitting with. Late night swiping is often not optimism. It is distraction from how you feel about the situation, and the app is a poor distraction because its content is the subject you are avoiding.

Sleep also pays for it. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine surveyed 2,007 US adults in 2025 and found most were prioritising screen time over sleep at bedtime.3

The pickup habit, which is the bit nobody names

There is a specific pattern worth recognising because it feels like nothing and accounts for a lot of the time.

You open the app, there is nothing new, you close it. Eleven seconds. No swiping, no reading, no decision. Then you do it again forty minutes later. None of those visits registers as use, which is why people are genuinely shocked by the figure in Settings.

That is not hope and it is not loneliness. It is a checking reflex, identical in shape to checking email, and it attaches to any app capable of delivering news about you. Notifications make it much worse, because every alert teaches the reflex that checking is sometimes rewarded, which is the most durable kind of learning there is.

Turning off notifications addresses that directly, and it is why it is the first recommendation on this page rather than the last.

Deleting versus blocking

For this category, deletion has better evidence behind it, at least by analogy.

A randomised study of 2,844 Facebook users found that four weeks of verified deactivation produced small but significant increases in wellbeing, more offline socialising, and a large persistent drop in use even after the experiment ended, with people valuing the service less than they had beforehand.4

Compare that with limiting. In a 2025 randomised trial, students who held phone use under two hours a day for three weeks improved on stress, wellbeing, depression and sleep, then returned towards baseline within six weeks of the limit ending.5

Those studies were not about dating apps. But the pattern is consistent: removing something changes how much you want it, while limiting it pauses the behaviour.

Delete the app rather than the account if you want to keep conversations. The habit is attached to the icon.

A fortnight off, done properly

If you are burnt out rather than compulsive, the useful version is a defined break with an end date, so it is not a decision you have to keep making.

Two weeks, apps deleted, accounts left intact. Tell anyone you are mid conversation with rather than disappearing on them. And decide in advance what you are going to do with the time, because the hours do not become anything by default. A randomised study found freed time went partly to socialising and partly to watching television alone.4

Start with this: turn off every notification from them, and block them from ten at night. Those two changes remove most of the use that was not chosen.

Block dating apps: FAQs

How do I block dating apps on my iPhone?

Set a Screen Time Schedule covering the evening and night, add the websites to Never Allow, and turn off every notification from them. The notifications usually matter most.

Should I delete dating apps or just limit them?

Deletion has better supporting evidence. A randomised deactivation study found a lasting drop in use afterwards, while limiting trials saw usage return towards baseline.

Why do I use dating apps most at night?

Your judgement is depleted, nothing is scheduled after it, and the alternative is lying in the dark with thoughts about the thing the app is about. It is distraction rather than optimism.

How do I know if my use is compulsive?

Check your pickup count in Settings, then Screen Time. If a dating app is among the first things you open each time you lift the phone, it is a feed habit rather than deliberate use.

How long should I take off?

Two weeks with a defined end date, so it is not a decision you keep making. Delete the apps but keep the accounts, and tell anyone you are mid conversation with.

Will blocking them make me feel better?

It removes the use you did not choose, which helps. If the problem is demoralisation rather than compulsion, a proper break will do more than a schedule.
