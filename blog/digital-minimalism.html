Digital minimalism, without the manifesto

The idea is sound and the online version of it has become a performance. Here is the part that holds up and how to actually start.

By Jacob Redshaw · 14 September 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer

Digital minimalism is the practice of deliberately choosing which technologies earn a place in your life and removing the rest, rather than reducing usage by willpower. The term was popularised by Cal Newport's 2019 book. It is a permanent design approach rather than a detox.

Digital minimalism is the practice of deciding which technologies genuinely earn a place in your life, removing the rest, and then not revisiting the decision every day. The term was popularised by Cal Newport's 2019 book of the same name.

The core idea is better than most self help because it is a design approach rather than a discipline approach. You are not trying to use things less. You are choosing fewer things.

Why digital minimalism works better than cutting down

Cutting down asks you to make a judgement every time. Removing something asks you once.

That difference is the whole thing. Phone use is roughly one decision per second for as long as the phone is in your hand, and you have to win every one of them. Subtraction changes the number of decisions rather than your success rate at them.

There is decent evidence for the subtraction version specifically. Allcott, Braghieri, Eichmeyer and Gentzkow randomly assigned a subset of 2,844 Facebook users to deactivate their accounts for four weeks, with the deactivation verified rather than self reported. They found small but significant increases in wellbeing, more offline socialising, and a large persistent drop in Facebook use after the experiment ended.1

That last finding is the interesting one. Four weeks away changed how much people wanted the thing, permanently. Compare that with limiting: in a 2025 randomised trial where 58 students held under two hours a day for three weeks, screen time climbed back towards baseline by the six week follow up.2

Removing appears to change the relationship. Limiting appears to pause it.

How to actually start, in 30 days

Not the full version with a philosophy attached. The version you will finish.

  1. List every app on your phone. All of them, on paper. It takes ten minutes and the length of the list is usually the first useful thing that happens.
  2. Mark each one as serves me, serves someone else, or neither. Be hard about it. Your banking app serves you. A feed usually serves whoever sells advertising on it. Both can be worth keeping, but name which is which.
  3. Delete everything in the neither category today. No deliberation. If you cannot say what it is for, it is not for anything.
  4. Remove the worst serves someone else app for 30 days. One. Not all of them.
  5. Decide what fills the gap first. You reach for it in queues, adverts and the ten minutes before bed. If nothing replaces those, you will reinstall by day four.
  6. At day 30, reinstall only what you genuinely missed. Most people find the list is shorter than they expected.

That is the usable core. Everything else in the genre is decoration.

Step two is where people cheat, so it is worth labouring. The question is not whether you enjoy an app. You enjoy plenty of things that are not serving you, and enjoyment is exactly what the design is optimising for. The question is whether, on a normal Tuesday, the app makes your life measurably better in a way you could describe to someone else. Messaging your friends passes that test easily. A feed you open forty times a day without remembering any of it does not, and the fact that you would miss it is evidence about the habit rather than about the value.

Step three catches more than people expect. Most phones carry a dozen things installed for one trip, one purchase or one afternoon, and each one sends notifications and occupies a small amount of attention forever afterwards. Clearing those is pure gain with nothing given up, which makes it the only part of this process that is genuinely free.

What digital minimalism gets wrong

Two things, and they are worth saying because the online version of this idea rarely admits either.

It assumes the choice is yours. Plenty of people cannot delete the app their work runs on, or the one their family group lives in, or the one their business sells through. For them the clean version is simply unavailable, and being told to delete it is useless advice. The practical answer there is making things unavailable at specific times rather than permanently, which is a timing problem rather than a minimalism problem.

It has become its own kind of status. A phone with four apps on a beige home screen is an aesthetic, and aesthetics are maintained in front of an audience. If the setup is something you post about, it has stopped being minimalism and become a hobby with a different shape. The test is whether anybody else ever notices.

There is also a real cost the research found. The same deactivation study reported reduced factual news knowledge among people who left Facebook.1 If a feed is your main source of information, you need a deliberate replacement rather than an assumption that news will reach you.

The honest expectation

Small but real improvements in how you feel. More time with people. Slightly worse awareness of current events unless you plan for it. A decent chance you use the removed thing much less even after it comes back.

Not a transformed personality. The effect sizes in the trials above are described as small, and small is what you should expect.

If you do one thing: delete every app you cannot explain the purpose of, today, without deliberating. It is the only step here with no downside.

Digital minimalism: FAQs

What is digital minimalism?

Deliberately choosing which technologies earn a place in your life and removing the rest, rather than trying to use everything a bit less. The term was popularised by Cal Newport's 2019 book.

Is digital minimalism the same as a digital detox?

No. A detox is temporary and ends, which is why usage tends to return to baseline. Minimalism is a permanent change to what exists on your phone in the first place.

How do I start digital minimalism?

List every app, mark each as serving you, serving someone else, or neither. Delete the neither pile today without deliberating, then remove your worst remaining app for 30 days.

Does digital minimalism actually work?

The subtraction approach has reasonable support. A randomised study of 2,844 Facebook users found four weeks of deactivation produced small wellbeing gains and a lasting drop in use afterwards.

What if I cannot delete apps I need for work?

Then make them unavailable at set times instead of permanently. That is a timing problem rather than a minimalism one, and the hours before bed are usually the cheapest to give up.

Is there a downside to digital minimalism?

One measured downside is reduced awareness of the news, found in the same deactivation study. Choose a deliberate news source before removing a feed you rely on for information.
