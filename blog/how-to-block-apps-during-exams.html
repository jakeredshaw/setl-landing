How to block apps during exams

Set this up in week one, not the week before. By the week before you have no capacity left for setting anything up.

By Jacob Redshaw · 14 September 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer

To block apps during exams, set a Screen Time Schedule covering your nights first, then use timed blocks of ninety minutes for revision sessions. Block the browser versions as well as the apps, and put the setup in place weeks before you need it.

The time to block apps during exams is about six weeks before them. By the week of, you are running on nothing and configuring a system is exactly the kind of task that will not happen.

Students are also the group this has genuinely been tested on, twice, which makes the advice here less speculative than usual.

Block the nights before the sessions

Counterintuitive and it is the right order.

Sleep is doing a large part of the consolidation work, and a bedtime block runs every night without you deciding anything. A revision block requires you to start it, and during exam season the days you most need it are the days you are least capable of initiating anything.

So set a Screen Time Schedule first, covering from half an hour before you want to sleep until an hour after you wake. Free, automatic, and it protects the thing that matters most.

Then strip your Always Allowed list to Phone and Messages, or the schedule will do nothing.

How to block apps during exams: the revision sessions

  1. Ninety minutes. Long enough to get past the uncomfortable start, short enough to face.
  2. Two a day, in the calendar, repeating. Scheduled in advance so starting one is not a decision you make while already avoiding work.
  3. Phone in another room for the session. Free and it does more than any app. A blocked phone in your hand is still a phone in your hand.
  4. Block the websites too. Blocking the app and using the site is the most common way this quietly fails. See how to block websites on iPhone.
  5. Keep paper for intrusions. Things will occur to you mid session. Writing them down lets you put them aside without opening anything.

What was actually measured on students

A 2025 randomised controlled trial in BMC Medicine took 111 healthy students with a mean age of 22.7 and a baseline of 276 minutes of phone use a day. 58 of them held under two hours a day for three weeks. Against the control group they improved on stress, wellbeing, depressive symptoms and sleep quality, with small to medium effect sizes.1

Note what was not measured: grades. Nobody has shown that blocking apps improves exam results, and anyone telling you a figure for that is inventing it. What the trial supports is lower stress and better sleep, which are worth having in an exam period on their own terms.

Then the finding that should shape your plan. Screen time climbed back quickly once the three weeks ended, and by the six week follow up was approaching baseline.1

That maps exactly onto how revision goes. You go hard for three weeks, it works, and then you return to normal. Fine for the exam, useless for the degree, which is why the night block should be permanent rather than seasonal.

There is a second study worth knowing about, which looked at this population at scale. Hjetland and colleagues studied 45,202 Norwegian students aged 18 to 28 and published in Frontiers in Psychiatry in 2025.2 It is observational, so it cannot establish causation, but it is a serious sample size in exactly your demographic, and it points the same direction as the trial.

The practical upshot of both is modest and worth stating plainly. Expect to feel less frazzled and sleep better, which in an exam period is genuinely valuable. Do not expect the blocking itself to be the reason you do well.

The failure mode specific to exam season

Blocking everything, all day, in a burst of panic three weeks out.

That version gets dismantled within days, usually for a legitimate reason, and once it is off it stays off for the whole period that mattered. A moderate arrangement you keep for two months beats a severe one you abandon in four days.

The second failure is subtler. If you are reaching for the phone every ninety seconds during one particular subject, that is information about the subject rather than about your discipline. Usually it means you do not know what the next step is, or you are avoiding finding out how much you do not know. No blocker touches either, and blocking apps while rereading highlighted notes for six hours just produces a quieter version of an ineffective method.

One practical warning about the week of the exams themselves. Do not change your setup then. Not to make it stricter, not to make it looser. Reconfiguring a system during the period it was built for is a very effective way of spending an afternoon feeling productive while revising nothing, and it almost always ends with the arrangement weaker than it was. Whatever you have in place by then is what you are going with.

Do this in week one: a permanent night block, two ninety minute sessions in your calendar, and your phone in another room during them. Then stop fiddling with the setup.

Block apps during exams: FAQs

How do I block apps during exam revision?

Set a permanent Screen Time Schedule for your nights first, then add two ninety minute revision blocks a day in your calendar. Block the websites as well as the apps.

Should I block my phone all day during exams?

No. All day blocks set in a panic get dismantled within days and then stay off. A moderate arrangement kept for two months beats a severe one abandoned in four days.

Night block or revision block first?

Night. Sleep does much of the consolidation work and a bedtime block runs automatically, whereas a revision block needs you to start it on days when you are least able to.

Will blocking apps improve my grades?

Nobody has shown that. The trials measured stress, wellbeing and sleep rather than results. Expect more usable study time, and fix your revision method as well.

When should I set this up?

Weeks before, ideally in week one of term. By the week of the exam you have no spare capacity for configuring anything, which is why late setups do not happen.

Why do I still avoid revising with everything blocked?

Usually because the next step is unclear or you are avoiding finding out what you do not know. Those produce identical reaching behaviour and no blocker addresses them.
