How to focus without your phone

Silencing it is not enough and face down is not enough. The object is the problem, not the notifications.

By Jacob Redshaw · 14 September 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer

To focus without your phone, put it in another room rather than silencing it or turning it face down. The object itself draws attention even when inert, because reaching for it is triggered by gaps in concentration rather than by notifications.

The standard advice on how to focus without your phone is to silence it and turn it face down. That is better than nothing and it misses what is actually happening.

Silencing removes the notifications. It leaves the object. And the object is most of the problem, because reaching for a phone is not usually triggered by an alert. It is triggered by a gap in your concentration, and gaps are produced by the work itself.

Why you reach for it when nothing has happened

Watch yourself during something difficult. The reach does not come when your phone buzzes. It comes at the exact moment the work gets uncomfortable: the paragraph that will not come out right, the bug you cannot locate, the point where you have to decide something.

That moment is not a failure of attention. It is the work getting hard, which is what work does. The phone is simply the nearest available exit, and taking an exit is easier than staying in the difficulty.

Which means notification settings are the wrong lever. You are not being interrupted. You are leaving.

That reframing is worth sitting with, because it changes what a solution has to do. If the problem were interruption, then silence would solve it, and for a lot of people silence demonstrably does not. If the problem is that you are taking an exit, then the only thing that helps is making the exit expensive enough that you notice yourself choosing it. Noticing is most of the work. Almost nobody decides to abandon a difficult paragraph for twenty minutes of nothing, and almost everybody does it.

How to focus without your phone, in order of what works

1. Put it in another room

Not in your bag. Not in a drawer. Another room, so retrieving it requires standing up and walking.

This is the whole thing, and everything below it is a distant second. A phone you have to walk for converts an impulse into a decision, and most impulses do not survive being turned into decisions.

2. Decide the session length before you start

Ninety minutes for hard cognitive work. Twenty five for admin. The number matters less than it existing, because a session with no end has no shape and you will check the time, which means checking the phone.

3. Write down the next action before you begin

Most reaching happens when you do not know what to do next. Vagueness produces exactly the same behaviour as boredom. One sentence on paper, specific enough to start on without thinking, removes a lot of the reaching before it happens.

4. Block the apps as well, not instead

If the phone has to stay in the room because you are on call or expecting something real, block your chosen apps for the session so calls still arrive and the feeds do not. This is the fallback, not the primary move.

5. Keep paper for the intrusions

Things will occur to you mid session. Book the dentist, reply to that email, check a fact. Writing them on paper lets you put them down without opening anything, which is what you were about to do.

About the 23 minute claim

You will see it everywhere: it takes 23 minutes and 15 seconds to recover from an interruption. Treat it carefully. The figure traces back to a small observational study of office workers rather than a controlled experiment on phone use, and the suspicious precision is doing work it has not earned.

What the evidence does support

Honestly: not much about productivity specifically. No trial has shown that blocking phone apps increases work output, and anybody claiming otherwise is selling you something.

What has been measured is the wellbeing side. A 2025 randomised controlled trial in BMC Medicine took 111 students averaging 276 minutes a day and had 58 hold under two hours a day for three weeks. Against the control they improved on stress, wellbeing, depressive symptoms and sleep quality, with small to medium effect sizes.1

Lower stress and better sleep are not nothing for concentration. But that is an indirect argument, and it is worth being clear that it is indirect rather than dressing it up as evidence about focus.

What this will not fix

If you are reaching for the phone every ninety seconds during one specific task, the phone is reporting something rather than causing it. Usually one of three things: you do not know what the next step is, you do not want to find out something the task will reveal, or you do not actually have authority to finish it.

No amount of blocking touches any of those, and treating them as discipline problems is how people end up with elaborate focus setups and no output. The useful question when you notice heavy reaching is not how do I resist this. It is what about this task am I avoiding.

Try this once: phone in another room, ninety minutes on a timer, next action written on paper before you start. If that does not work, the problem is the task rather than the phone.

Focus without your phone: FAQs

Is it enough to put my phone face down?

Not really. Face down removes the notifications and leaves the object, and the object is most of the problem, since reaching is triggered by gaps in concentration rather than by alerts.

Where should I put my phone while working?

Another room, so getting it requires standing up and walking. A bag or a drawer is still within impulse range. The walk is what turns an impulse into a decision.

How long should a focus session be?

Ninety minutes for hard cognitive work, twenty five for admin. What matters most is that an end exists, because a session with no end leads you to check the time on your phone.

Does blocking apps make you more productive?

No trial has shown that. What has been measured is that reducing phone use improves stress, mood and sleep modestly, which helps concentration indirectly rather than directly.

Why do I reach for my phone when nothing has happened?

Because the work just got difficult. The reach usually arrives at the exact moment a task becomes uncomfortable, and the phone is the nearest available exit from that discomfort.

What if I still cannot focus with my phone in another room?

Then the phone was not the problem. Usually it means the next step is unclear, you are avoiding what the task will reveal, or you cannot actually finish it without someone else.
