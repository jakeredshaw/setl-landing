How to block news apps on iPhone

Worth doing, with one genuine caveat that most advice on this subject never mentions.

By Jacob Redshaw · 14 September 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answer

To block news apps on iPhone, set a Screen Time Schedule covering the News category and add the matching websites to Never Allow. Before you do, pick a replacement news source, because one randomised study found that cutting a feed reduced factual news knowledge.

You can block news apps on iPhone in about three minutes. The more interesting question is whether you should, and what to put in the gap.

The caveat first, because nobody else mentions it

A randomised study of 2,844 Facebook users measured what happened when a subset deactivated for four weeks, verified rather than self reported. They found small but significant increases in wellbeing, more offline socialising and reduced political polarisation.1

They also found reduced factual news knowledge.1

That is a real cost, and being uninformed is not a form of wellbeing. Any page telling you to quit the news without mentioning a downside is selling rather than informing.

So the useful framing is not that news is bad for you. It is that an endless feed is a poor delivery mechanism for it. Change the delivery, keep the interest.

How to block news apps on iPhone

  1. Open Settings, then Screen Time.
  2. Tap Screen Time Schedule and turn it on.2
  3. Select the News category rather than individual apps.
  4. Add the websites too, under Content and Privacy Restrictions, then Web Content, then Never Allow.3 News is the category where the website substitutes most easily for the app.
  5. Turn off every news notification. This matters more here than in any other category.
  6. Strip your Always Allowed list, especially any browser.

The notification step deserves emphasis. Breaking news alerts are designed to interrupt, and they convert a decision to read the news into a reflex you did not choose. Turning them off changes more than the block does.

What to replace it with

Pick something with an ending. That is the whole principle.

  • A newspaper, print or an app edition that is published once and finishes.
  • A daily or weekly newsletter. Arrives once, ends.
  • A radio or television bulletin. Fifteen minutes and over.
  • A weekly magazine. Fewer events, more explanation, and you will understand more than you did from forty minutes of fragments.

Any of these leaves you better informed than a feed, which is the counterintuitive part. A feed is optimised for the next item rather than for a complete account, so you are given an unending supply of fragments, each implying a picture you never reach.

How much news is actually enough

Worth asking honestly, because for most people the answer is far less than they currently consume.

Almost nothing you read in a feed changes a decision you will make. In the rare cases where it would, it will still be there in the evening bulletin or tomorrow's paper. The urgency is a property of the delivery rather than of the information.

A useful test: of everything you read yesterday, how much changed what you did, and how much can you even recall. If the honest answer is almost none and almost nothing, then cutting the volume by three quarters costs you nothing except the feeling of being up to date, which is not the same thing as being informed.

That is also how this reconciles with the knowledge finding above. Losing a feed reduced factual knowledge in that study, and a once daily source with an ending addresses that directly, which a feed only appeared to.

Why news feeds are specifically hard to leave

Three mechanisms, and none of them are a weakness in you.

Threat gets attentional priority. That is a sensible default for an animal that needs to notice danger, and applied to an infinite feed of global events it means the alarming item always wins.

Uncertainty feels solvable. You are not reading bad news for pleasure. You are looking for the piece of information that makes the situation make sense. It does not exist, so the search has no end.

It feels responsible. Unlike scrolling a social feed, reading the news comes with a justification, which makes it much harder to stop and much easier to defend to yourself.

That third one is why news resists blocking more than almost anything else. You can always argue you were being a citizen.

If your work requires it

Some people genuinely need to follow events closely, and for them a blanket block is bad advice. The useful version is separating working hours from the rest, which is a timing problem rather than a content one.

Block the category from the evening through to the morning and leave the working day untouched. You keep the professional requirement and lose the part that was costing you sleep, and nothing about your job needs you reading about a crisis at midnight. The practical setup is in the Screen Time Schedule guide.

What cutting back does

A 2025 randomised controlled trial in BMC Medicine took 111 students averaging 276 minutes of phone a day and had 58 hold under two hours a day for three weeks, with improvements in stress, wellbeing, depressive symptoms and sleep quality against a control group.4

Usage returned towards baseline once the limit ended.4 So pick something permanent and small rather than a dramatic cut.

Do it in this order: pick your replacement news source first, then turn off every news notification, then block the category for the evening.

Block news apps: FAQs

How do I block news apps on my iPhone?

Set a Screen Time Schedule covering the News category, add the matching websites to Never Allow, and turn off every news notification. The notifications usually matter more than the block.

Is quitting the news bad for you?

There is a measured cost. A randomised study found that people who deactivated Facebook for four weeks knew measurably less about current events, so choose a replacement source first.

What should I replace a news feed with?

Something with an ending. A newspaper, a daily newsletter, a radio bulletin or a weekly magazine. You will typically understand more than you did from a feed of fragments.

Why is it so hard to stop reading the news?

Threat gets attentional priority, uncertainty feels solvable so the search has no end, and reading the news feels responsible, which makes it easy to justify and hard to stop.

Should I turn off breaking news alerts?

Yes, and it is probably the highest value change here. Alerts convert a decision to read the news into a reflex you did not choose.

Is blocking the app enough?

No. News is the category where the website substitutes most easily, so add the sites to Never Allow and remove any browser from your Always Allowed list.
